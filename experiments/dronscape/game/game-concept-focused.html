Focused design

Countermeasure

Every drone you field teaches the enemy what to jam next.

Elevator pitch

You command a drone detachment on an open, fog-covered fictional front. It starts as three commercial quadcopters and grows, chapter by chapter, into hundreds of air and ground drones, following the published scale curve of the 2022–2026 drone war. You don't build a base. Each mission you pick a loadout from a supply budget, give every flight a role card and a few if-then behavior rules, and run the mission in real time with pause. You only see what your sensors see. Scouts, relays, ground masts, and night thermal sensors push back the fog, but only while their links hold. When a jammer bites, orders stop arriving, that patch of map goes dark, and drones fall back on the last order you gave them. Completing objectives earns Lessons that unlock the next generation of drone technology, and the enemy adapts to whatever you lean on. As your numbers grow and your command tokens don't, the game forces you to stop steering drones and start writing doctrine. It is told abstractly, with no real places, units, or people.

Core loop

  1. Brief: an open fictional map with fog, 1 primary and up to 2 optional objectives, known and unknown EW zones, a day/night clock, and a supply budget.
  2. Loadout: spend supply on drones from your unlocked roster. No base, nothing carried over.
  3. Assign: group drones into flights. Each flight gets a role card, a route or sector, and 1–3 if-then rules, one of which must be the Link-Lost rule.
  4. Run: on an open, fog-covered map in real time with pause, 8–20 minutes. You hold 3–4 live command tokens. Jamming degrades links and blinds sensors, and drones fall back on their rules.
  5. Debrief: an after-action timeline of orders tasked versus delivered, link deaths, and silent behavior. You earn Lessons, and the enemy adapts to what you used.
  6. Research: spend Lessons on the next tier. Some tiers only open after the enemy fields the counter they answer.

Open map and fog of war

Countermeasure is played on a continuous map, not a grid. The fog of war is the comms model made visible.

  • Open, continuous map: A top-down 2D map with no grid, hexes, or tiles. Drones move freely across a fictional valley or coastline, and ground robots follow roads, tracks, and slopes. It runs in real time with pause: Space pauses, you can queue orders while paused, and speed steps through 1×, 2×, and 4×.
  • Fog comes from sensors, not a vision radius: The map has three layers. Seen now is live coverage from your sensors. Known is what you saw earlier, drawn desaturated with a timestamp. Unknown is fog. Coverage comes from Scout Quads (day camera cones), Relay Wings (a small airborne picket), Relay Mast UGVs (wide ground coverage, blocked by ridges), and thermal sensors on Night Heavies, which reach farther after dark. Enemy transmitters also show up as bearing lines rather than exact positions.
  • Fog depends on the link: A drone only shares what it sees while its link is up. On Clear, coverage is live. On Degraded, its feed lags and shows a stale timestamp. On Lost, its patch of map goes dark, even though the drone still sees and acts on its own rules. Jamming therefore blinds you as well as cutting your orders, which ties the fog straight to the vignette's thesis.
  • Last-known-position ghosts: An enemy contact that leaves coverage becomes a ghost icon with a timestamp. Its uncertainty circle grows with the contact's speed class, and the ghost fades after about 60 seconds. Your own drones get the same treatment when their links drop: you see where they were, not where they are. Decoys can create ghosts of things that were never there.
  • Comms and jam bubbles on the map: Your comms bubble is a soft blue field from the Operator Post, and Relays and Masts extend it. Once a jammer is found, its bubble appears as a red dashed ring. Until then, you infer the bubble from a link-loss heat map showing where your drones dropped out.
  • Terrain and line of sight: Ridges, buildings, and forest cast both comms shadows and sensor shadows. Climbing restores a link but raises your signature. Forest slows ground robots but hides them. When you place a Relay or Mast, a line-of-sight preview shows the coverage it would give before you commit.
  • Day, night, and weather: Dusk is a scheduled event. At night, daylight camera cones shrink and thermal coverage grows. Rain and smoke cut both, as an abstract penalty.
  • Strategic zoom and exception alerts: Zooming out collapses drones into flight and group glyphs that show strength and link state, in the style of Supreme Commander's strategic zoom. In late chapters this is the default view. An event feed ('Mule-3 lost link 1:12', 'Ghost expiring: recon contact') jumps the camera to whatever needs a human.

Role and behavior system

Role cards

A drone only accepts roles it can perform.

  • Scout: Sweep a sector and reveal enemies, jam zones, and obstacles.
  • Spot: Hold over a marked target so Fire Support or Strike can act on it.
  • Strike: Attack a designated enemy equipment or node target (abstract, no gore).
  • Escort: Stay with a friendly unit and react to threats against it.
  • Intercept: Patrol an air lane and engage enemy drones.
  • Relay: Orbit or park to extend the comms bubble.
  • Resupply: Carry cargo along a route to a drop point.
  • Recover: Retrieve a crew pod or a downed friendly drone.
  • Obstacle: Lay or clear barriers and minefields.
  • Hold: Occupy and defend a point.
  • Jam: Project a jam bubble. It hurts both sides' radio drones.

Behavior rules (gambit-style)

Priority-ordered, limited by slots: 1 at the start (the Link-Lost rule is required), +1 at T4, and +1 at T10.

IF link LOST → Hold position | Continue last order | Return home | Climb to reconnect | Seek nearest relay | Self-guide to locked target (needs T10 chip)
IF link DEGRADED → Slow down | Climb | Continue
IF enemy drone spotted → Report | Evade to cover | Engage (Interceptor/Escort only)
IF battery < 30% → Return | Swap at Mule | Finish current leg
IF escorted unit attacked → Engage | Screen | Pull back with it
IF night → Activate (Night Heavy waits for dark) | Go to ground
IF cargo delivered / target done → Return | Hold | Next waypoint

Comms and jamming as the core mechanic

  • Comms bubble: Your Operator Post projects a radio bubble. Relay Wings and Masts extend it, and ridges and buildings cast comms shadows.
  • Three link states (straight from the vignette): Clear: orders arrive at once. Degraded: orders are delayed or dropped and the drone's video goes stale (its feed timestamp goes stale and its icon fades). Lost: the drone runs its Link-Lost rule, and you only see its last known fix.
  • Command tokens: You get about 3 live orders, regenerating over time. Orders sent into degraded zones cost more and can fail. Good planning means needing fewer live orders.
  • Emissions: Every transmission raises your signature. Talk too much and enemy direction-finding reveals your Operator Post, which is a mission-fail risk.
  • Counters that cut both ways: Your Jammer UGV blinds your own radio drones too. Fiber ignores jamming but drags a cable. Autonomy chips keep going without you, so they can also lock onto a decoy.
  • After-action timeline: After every mission you see a scrub bar of orders tasked versus delivered, which links died where, and what each drone did while it was silent. This is the vignette's replay strip, scored.

Scale curve: following the real war's published counts

Ukraine's Air Force publishes daily counts of Russian long-range attack drones, and governments and analysts publish yearly production and monthly usage figures. Together they give a pacing guide: the war started with a handful of commercial quadcopters and grew to hundreds of launches a night and millions of drones a year. The campaign borrows that shape, scaled down hard.

Gaps, stated honestly
  • There's no consistent official daily or monthly series for FPV or front-line drone use. The monthly front-line ranges are RUSI estimates.
  • There's no official monthly Shahed breakdown for 2023 or the first half of 2024. The 2023 rate is derived from a cumulative total.
  • AF counts cover overnight windows (about 6 PM to 8 AM) and are described as approximate. Daytime waves are undercounted: July 2026 shows 4,891 in AF reports against 8,653 from the MoD.
  • Production figures mix procurement data, official claims, and targets. Volunteer-bought commercial drones, which dominated 2022, aren't in the official totals.
  • Russian MoD shoot-down claims are unverified. They're listed to show the scale on the other side and aren't used in the curve.

How real counts become game counts

  • Your drones per mission ≈ Ukraine's annual drone deliveries ÷ 40,000, interpolated within each year. 2023's 415k gives about 10, 2024's roughly 2M gives about 50, 2025's 3M gives about 75, and 2026's 6–8M gives 150–200, with the finale capped at 250. Chapter 1 uses a floor of 3–5 because the 2022 official figure (1,200) leaves out the volunteer-bought commercial quadcopters that actually dominated that year.
  • Enemy wave per mission ≈ the real average daily Shahed-type launches nationwide ÷ 3. Late 2022's roughly 6 a day gives 2, mid-2024's roughly 14 a day gives about 5, Mar 2025's roughly 135 a day gives about 45, and May 2026's 263 a day gives about 88. Record nights become set pieces: 810 ÷ 3 ≈ 270 and 948 ÷ 3 ≈ 315.
  • Why two divisors? The two series measure different things: nationwide long-range strike launches versus all-type production. Each gets its own divisor so that it lands in a playable range of 2 to 300. The game borrows each curve's shape (enemy waves grow about 45× and your force about 16× after the 2022 floor), never a ratio between the two sides. Real numbers are only a pacing guide, so a designer can flatten any step that doesn't play well.
  • Escalation inside a mission (borrowed from Steel Division 2's battle phases): later chapters release reinforcement waves on a timer, so each mission replays the chapter's ramp in miniature.

Why scale forces delegation

  • Command tokens stay roughly flat at 3–4 live orders while your drone count grows from about 4 to about 250. In Chapter 1 that's about one order per drone. By Chapter 6 it's one order per 50–80 drones.
  • So the game teaches delegation the way the war forced it. You stop being able to steer drones, then flights, then squadrons. What still works is what you set up in advance: role cards, if-then rules, doctrine templates, standing sector orders, and finally autonomy chips. Each chapter's unlocks are the tools that make the next jump in scale manageable.
  • The interface changes along with the scale. Direct waypoints work in Chapters 1–2. Flight cards and rule slots carry Chapters 2–4. Doctrine templates (one role plus rules, stamped onto many flights) and sector ownership arrive in Chapters 4–5. Chapter 6 is played mostly from the strategic zoom, with exception alerts. The skill shifts from micro to doctrine design, and the after-action timeline becomes the main way to improve.
  • Failure teaches the lesson: bring Chapter 2 habits (hand-steering) into Chapter 4 and you run out of tokens while flights sit idle or drift. The debrief shows exactly which flights had no rule for the moment they lost contact.

How it plays

What makes it fun

  • Plan, then watch, with tension. The fun is predicting how your swarm will behave when you go silent, then seeing whether your doctrine holds. It's the Gratuitous Space Battles and Door Kickers pleasure applied to drones.
  • Fog you earn and lose. Every patch of map you can see is a drone that's still talking to you. Watching the fog creep back in as links drop, with ghosts fading where contacts used to be, makes jamming something you feel.
  • From three quadcopters to a battle group. The campaign's sense of escalation follows the real war's published curve, so early missions feel intimate and late missions feel overwhelming, and that's on purpose.
  • A rock-paper-scissors that evolves. Every counter is real-inspired and every unlock answers a specific enemy move, so the tech tree reads as a story about the drone war rather than a list of stats.
  • Failures you can read. The after-action strip and link-loss heat map turn every loss into a lesson ('my convoy never got the reroute'), which makes 'one more try' feel fair.
  • Adaptation forces variety. Any single dominant strategy gets countered next mission, so players have to keep evolving, which mirrors the war itself.
  • Pause-friendly sessions. Missions run 8–20 minutes and get longer in late chapters. Real time with pause means you're never overwhelmed for long, and there's no base to manage.

Variant angles

Variant A: 'Last Order' puzzle mode (browser-first, closest to the vignette)

Fixed loadouts and hand-made maps. You're scored on objectives, live orders used, and drones still in contact, with Opus Magnum-style histograms. It reuses the vignette's agent model, jam toggle, and replay strip almost as-is.

Variant B: 'Red Cell' asymmetric 1v1

One player builds the loadout and rules. The other spends a counter budget from the same era: jammers, nets, interceptors, decoys, mines. Both watch a shared, fog-free after-action replay.

Tone guardrails

  • Fictional factions, maps, and place names. No real places, units, or people appear in the game, and the real-war eras live only in the design notes and an optional codex.
  • Targets are equipment, nodes, routes, and obstacles. Casualties are abstracted: units are 'disabled' and crews are 'recovered'. No gore.
  • All stats are abstract 1–5 ratings. Nothing in the game gives real technical, build, or targeting detail.
  • Mission types lean toward resupply, recovery, recon, defense, and disabling enemy equipment.

Platform scope

Browser-first, as a 2D top-down canvas. Its agent model, jam sector, link states, and replay strip can be lifted from the existing Dronscape vignette. Fog can be rendered as an offscreen coverage mask, and a few hundred simple agents is well within canvas limits. A full six-chapter campaign with around 25 missions is a solid indie scope. Variant A alone, at Chapter 1–2 scale, is the cheapest slice.

Caveats
  • I summarized real-war facts at capability level from the cited reporting and analysis. Numbers such as RUSI's ~10,000/month and CSIS's 10–20% → 70–80% are estimates from those sources, not independently verified.
  • I couldn't fetch the Reuters 'kill zone' article directly (paywall or bot block). Its cited claim comes from Reuters' own text as quoted in search results.
  • The Forbes Hambling mothership piece wouldn't load, so the mothership tier cites The War Zone and Business Insider instead.
  • The Ukraine MoD (Vampire) page is a primary but interested source, so I paired it with Forbes.
  • Broken Arrow's Steam page doesn't mention drones, but the beginner's guide clip shows an RQ-4 Global Hawk in the deck builder's recon tab. It's still listed mainly for joint air-ground play and decks.
  • Gameplay clips are fan and press tutorials and let's-plays, not official footage. They show the mechanic clearly but can be older builds, and UI may differ from the current release.
  • Command: Modern Operations: the clip shows the line-of-sight tool and sensor range rings. It doesn't clearly show contact uncertainty ellipses or autonomous missions, so those parts of 'what to borrow' still rest on the game's own description.
  • Cold Waters: the clip shows the 2D tactical plot with contact tracks, but bearing lines aren't prominent, and the narrator notes the game does most target motion analysis for you.
  • HighFleet: kept in the main list (not demoted) because the 'Tip 2: Sensors' segment shows the strategic map with radar rings and ELINT for about a minute, and the narration describes the emissions trade-off directly. Its trailer is still mostly side-view combat, so use the gameplay link.
  • DEFCON: the clip shows the minimalist map and icons; radar circles aren't prominent at that moment.
  • Broken Arrow and Command: Modern Operations also have a second timestamp; only one still per game was saved.
  • FPV Kamikaze Drone links to its Steam page because I didn't verify a YouTube trailer.
  • Shahed-type counts are nationwide long-range strikes, not front-line sector numbers. I use them as the enemy pressure index because they're the only consistent official daily series, which makes the curve a pacing guide rather than a simulation.
  • AF daily counts are approximate and cover overnight windows, so daytime launches are undercounted (July 2026: 4,891 in AF reports against 8,653 from the MoD). The 2023 monthly rate is derived from a cumulative total, and early-2024 months aren't broken out.
  • Production numbers mix procurement data, official claims, and targets. The 2026 figures (6–7M expected, 8M+ capacity) are forward-looking statements. I couldn't fetch the Reuters July 2026 article directly, so that number comes from search excerpts, and the NSDC page is the primary source.
  • Front-line FPV usage has no official daily series. The RUSI 'tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands per month' figure is an estimate.
  • Russian MoD shoot-down claims are unverified and aren't used in the curve.
  • Both divisors (÷40,000 of annual deliveries for your drones, ÷3 of the daily launch average for enemy waves) are design choices tuned for play, not derived truths.
  • AP, Reuters, and France 24 pages return bot-block codes (401/403) to scripted checks. These are access blocks, not dead links.
  • Supreme Commander links to its Steam page because the only trailer I found was a fan upload. Men of War II's trailer is on its publisher's channel, and the Regiments trailer is on GameTrailers.
