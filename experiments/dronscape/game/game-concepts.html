Dronscape: game concept brainstorm

Drafted Oct 1, 2026 for Richard Bailey · 8 concepts · brainstorm only

Scope note. Expanding into games is outside the current Dronscape direction lock (Track 1: supervisory C2 vignette). These concepts are a brainstorm, not a build plan. Building any of them would require Richard to rewrite direction-lock.md first.

Thesis these riff on: a supervisor tasks a small swarm (8–30 agents) at mission level, the swarm keeps behaving when comms degrade or get jammed, and the operator can see what broke. Live vignette.

Top picks

(a) Strongest as a fun standalone game

  1. Relay Age: Age of Empires, except your empire ends where your signal does.
    Answers the AoE request directly and adds one new rule (territory = signal) that changes every classic RTS decision.
  2. Derelict Signal: Every drone you lose is a voice that stops answering.
    Uses the proven FTL/Duskers roguelite loop, where losing drones you can't replace gives every run real stakes.
  3. Ember Watch: Smoke eats your signal, and the fire doesn't wait.
    A broad-audience, tasteful premise with a simulation that naturally generates drama, and it can be cozy or tense depending on the season.

(b) Most natural extension of the browser vignette

  1. Last Order: You get three messages. Make the swarm's silence count.
    Turns the vignette's jam toggle, last-order autonomy, and after-action strip into scored puzzle levels almost one-for-one.
  2. Handler vs. Noise: One player commands the swarm. The other player is the noise.
    Makes the jam toggle a human opponent and the replay a shared post-match moment, without adding new systems to the vignette's agent model.
  3. Ember Watch: Smoke eats your signal, and the fire doesn't wait.
    Fits the existing foothills-grassland basemap and the 16-agent survey tasking, with smoke standing in for the jammer.

RTS base-building (AoE-like)

1.Relay Age

Age of Empires, except your empire ends where your signal does.

Elevator pitch

You found a frontier settlement in a fictional river valley, and drones are both your villagers and your army. Relay masts work like town centers: they claim territory, and inside their coverage you can give orders the normal RTS way. Outside it, your units run on the last order you gave and a small menu of fallback behaviors. You advance through ages by comms tier (line-of-sight radio, then mesh, then high-altitude relay). A rival faction expands the same way and tries to jam you out of your own map.

Core loop
  • Build: harvester drones gather ore, solar, and timber; you place relay masts, charging pads, and fabricators.
  • Patrol: you set escort and picket routes that keep running when a sector goes dark.
  • Manage: you split power between production and coverage, and choose which age-up to buy.
What makes it fun

Expanding past your relay coverage is greedy. Remote resource fields pay well, but drones sent there are on autopilot, and a rival jammer can freeze a whole economic wing. The question in every game is whether you build the mast first or send the miners first.

Comms / jamming as a mechanic

Coverage is your territory. Jamming is a siege weapon. Last-order autonomy is how your units behave by default whenever they lose the link. After each match, a replay highlights which orders never arrived, a direct reuse of Dronscape's "see what broke" idea.

Platform scope

Bigger than a browser prototype. A 2D/2.5D browser skirmish against one AI on one map is doable as a slice, but a full campaign with multiplayer is a real studio-scale RTS.

2.Perimeter Hours

Draw the patrol loop at dusk, then watch it hold, or fray, until dawn.

Elevator pitch

You look after a fictional wildlife preserve and research station with 8 to 20 patrol drones. By day you draw patrol loops, place sensor posts, and tune battery swaps. By night, abstract incursions (stray rogue drones, storms, sensor-blind ravines) test the plan while you watch, step in, and re-task. Every night is short and readable, so you can always see why a gap opened.

Core loop
  • Build: sensor posts, charging cradles, and relay poles.
  • Patrol: draw loops, set dwell times, and assign overlap.
  • Manage: battery rotations, wear, and a small upgrade budget each day.
What makes it fun

Coverage, endurance, and reserve pull against each other. Tight loops leave no drones in reserve. Wide loops leave holes. Every drone on the charger is a gap somewhere on the map.

Comms / jamming as a mechanic

Storm cells and interference pockets cut links mid-loop. Drones keep flying the last loop they were given, but they can't be pulled off it to answer an alarm. The morning report shows coverage minute by minute and marks where orders went stale.

Platform scope

Browser-first. Top-down 2D canvas, short sessions, and possibly a daily-challenge seed.

3.Last Mile Valley

Mini Metro for drone delivery, where every hop is a bet on the battery.

Elevator pitch

A fictional valley of orchards, ranches, and small towns needs parcels, medical supplies, and crop-scouting flights. You draw a network of pads, charging hubs, and corridors, and the fleet runs it on its own. Demand grows, weather rolls in, and orchards need survey flights at harvest time. The challenge is to keep the network flowing as it grows past what you can micromanage.

Core loop
  • Build: pads, hubs, and battery-swap stations.
  • Patrol: seasonal crop-survey sweeps that compete with deliveries for airframes.
  • Manage: throughput, battery state, and an upgrade token each week, Mini Metro style.
What makes it fun

You have to plan ahead under growth pressure. Each new town or orchard contract strains the network, and the pleasure is in rerouting cleverly rather than adding more drones.

Comms / jamming as a mechanic

Dead zones in canyons and wind shadows mean drones have to carry their routing with them. You decide which hubs keep a local copy of the dispatch table so a sector can keep running when it loses the uplink. A comms outage doesn't end a run. It slowly drains it.

Platform scope

Browser-first and very achievable: minimalist 2D vector art and one-handed play, which also suits mobile.

Contested-comms / EW puzzle

4.Last Order

You get three messages. Make the swarm's silence count.

Elevator pitch

Each level is a small map, a swarm of 8 to 16 agents, a mission objective, and jam fields that switch on at known (or unknown) times. Every order costs bandwidth and you have very little, so you mostly author fallback behaviors ("if link lost: hold / continue / return / relay-chain") before you press Go. Then you watch the run play out. Levels are scored on completion, messages used, and agents still in contact, and solutions can be shared as replays.

Core loop
  • Build: compose per-flight orders and fallback rules from a small set of logic tiles.
  • Patrol: run the mission, watch links degrade, and spend scarce live messages.
  • Manage: optimize for minimal messages and robustness to jam timing, then compare on a histogram like Opus Magnum's.
What makes it fun

You try to predict how the swarm will behave in the silence. The best solutions need almost no live command, which is the Dronscape thesis turned into a scoring rule.

Comms / jamming as a mechanic

Comms degradation is the whole game. Jam toggles, degraded links that drop or delay packets, lost-link fallbacks, and the after-action strip that shows tasked versus delivered are all taken directly from the existing vignette.

Platform scope

Browser-first. Could reuse the vignette's agent model, jam sector, and replay strip almost as-is. Levels can be small JSON files.

5.Ember Watch

Smoke eats your signal, and the fire doesn't wait.

Elevator pitch

You run a volunteer drone unit through a fire season in fictional golden foothills. In the calm stretches you clear fuel breaks, site lookout relays, and train new airframes. When a fire starts, you task spotter drones to map the edge, direct water and retardant tenders, and keep evacuation corridors watched while the wind turns. The fire is a simulation (fuel, slope, wind), not an enemy, and success means acres saved and everyone out safely.

Core loop
  • Build: lookout relays, fuel breaks, and water points between incidents.
  • Patrol: spotter sweeps and edge-mapping during an incident.
  • Manage: drone hours, retardant stock, crew fatigue, and the season's budget.
What makes it fun

You choose between going after the head of the fire and protecting the flank, with less information than you'd like, while the wind keeps changing what the right call is.

Comms / jamming as a mechanic

Smoke plumes and terrain weaken links, and the fire's front can cut off relays. Drones inside the smoke act on their last tasking, and the fire map ages visibly until a drone gets back in contact.

Platform scope

Browser-first slice on one valley map, with room to grow into a full indie game (seasons and a campaign).

Cozy civilian: search and rescue

6.Canyon Beacon

Somewhere in these canyons someone is waiting, and every relay you drop gets you closer.

Elevator pitch

A hiker is overdue in a fictional canyon park. You start with a probability heat map (last-seen point, terrain, weather) and a handful of drones with thermal and visual sensors. Canyons block signal, so you build a daisy chain of relay drones to push the search deeper, and that chain is also the only way your scouts' findings get back to you. It's calm, kind, and rewards careful thinking.

Core loop
  • Build: lay a relay chain and choose staging points.
  • Patrol: fly search patterns (parallel, expanding square, contour) and set sensor modes.
  • Manage: battery, daylight, and how you weight the probability map as clues come in.
What makes it fun

Every relay you place is a scout you aren't using to search. You're trading reach against search area while the light fades.

Comms / jamming as a mechanic

Terrain-shadowed links are the core mechanic. A scout beyond the chain keeps searching under its last order but can't report a find until it returns or the chain catches up. The debrief replays exactly when each clue could have reached you.

Platform scope

Browser-first: short scenarios, a procedural canyon generator, and a good fit for a relaxing daily puzzle.

Roguelite (sci-fi salvage)

7.Derelict Signal

Every drone you lose is a voice that stops answering.

Elevator pitch

Your tender ship drifts through procedurally generated derelict stations in deep space. Each stop, you send a small squad of salvage drones in through flaky ship systems, with mission-level orders only: sweep, salvage, seal doors, and fall back. Interference grows the deeper you go, and drones may finish their order alone in the dark or never come back. Between stops you repair, upgrade, and decide which drones' memory cores are worth the scrap.

Core loop
  • Build: fit drones with modules between runs (scanner, cutter, relay, shield).
  • Patrol: explore room by room, issuing mission-level tasks.
  • Manage: fuel, scrap, and drone wear, plus which risky detours to take.
What makes it fun

The FTL and Duskers kind of tension: you push greedy for loot with a fragile, irreplaceable crew, and losing one hurts.

Comms / jamming as a mechanic

Station interference gets worse the deeper you go. Lost drones keep executing their last task, and you can sometimes find them later. An end-of-run log reconstructs which orders were heard and what each drone did after it went silent.

Platform scope

Browser-first is feasible with 2D top-down rooms and an optional terminal-style command line (a nod to Duskers). A premium indie version would need more.

Asymmetric multiplayer (1v1)

8.Handler vs. Noise

One player commands the swarm. The other player is the noise.

Elevator pitch

A short, abstract 1v1 match. The Handler has 12 to 16 drones and a mission (survey five waypoints, then deliver and return). The Noise has a budget of jammers, decoys, weather cells, and spoofed beacons, and sees only coarse telemetry. The Handler plans for robustness and the Noise looks for weak points. After the match, both players watch the same after-action replay with fog removed, which is half the fun.

Core loop
  • Build: the Handler composes flights and fallbacks, and the Noise drafts an interference loadout.
  • Patrol: a real-time match of about 6 to 10 minutes.
  • Manage: the Handler spends bandwidth and the Noise spends budget and timing.
What makes it fun

It's a mind game. A jammer is strongest when it's unexpected, and a swarm is strongest when it doesn't need the link. The Handler can bait out jammers and the Noise can bait out panicked re-tasking.

Comms / jamming as a mechanic

The vignette's jam toggle becomes the opponent. Everything that makes the thesis interesting (last-order autonomy, stale fixes, failed orders, the replay) becomes something to compete over.

Platform scope

Medium. The game logic fits a browser, but real-time netcode, matchmaking, and balance add a lot. Hot-seat or play-by-replay versus AI would be a cheap first step.
