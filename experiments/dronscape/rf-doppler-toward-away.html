RF Doppler: toward or away

Nov 1, 2026 · for Richard Bailey · a research note, not a build. Standalone page. Nothing here changes the Track 1 direction lock.

Thesis. The sign of a tracked frequency change says whether a detected drone emission is closing or opening. The size of that change is a rough radial speed. Absolute frequency is mostly a statement about the clocks.

Scope. Track 1–adjacent parked research: could a supervisor get an “emitter closing” cue from a signal the aircraft is already transmitting, with no active radar? This page is a learning path and a garage test plan. It is outside building the C2 vignette until you ask.

Skim

What we want. Three things only: toward, away, and a rough radial speed. Radial means the part of the velocity aimed straight at the receiver or straight away from it. A full position, a bearing, and “is it a drone?” are other problems.

One-way intuition. The drone’s own transmitter is a stack of wave crests. Fly toward the receiver and the crests arrive more often, so the tone sits higher. Fly away and they arrive less often, so the tone sits lower. At multirotor speeds, about 5–15 m/s, the shift on a 2.4 or 5.8 GHz carrier is tens to a few hundred hertz. You see it as a peak moving on a waterfall, if the receiver’s own clock holds still enough to let an 80 Hz step show.

Why the change matters. Both crystals are wrong by parts per million. One part per million at 2.4 GHz is 2,400 Hz. The whole Doppler shift at 10 m/s is about 80 Hz. A single reading of the absolute frequency cannot say who moved. The useful number is the change from a moment when the emitter was still, or the difference between two receivers that share a clock.

When a low-cost SDR can see it. Software can make a 10 Hz bin. A warmed-up single dongle can show the sign on a clean continuous tone, inside a band it can actually tune, if you record a rest tone first and the run is short. It loses the sign on a hopping digital link, on a wide FM video carrier whose deviation is megahertz, and on a crossing pass, where the radial speed is about zero even though the aircraft is close and fast. A GPS-disciplined clock, or two coherent channels, is what makes the sign trustworthy enough to put in a product.

SDR means a software-defined radio: the computer does the tuning and the measurement. The numbers above use c ≈ 3.00×108 m/s and are estimates, not lab results. No flight test has been run for this note.

Math and RF theory

Inset · teach this first

For one-way listening (we hear the drone’s transmitter, we do not bounce a pulse off it), the frequency shift is

Δf ≈ f · vradial / c

Take vradial as positive when the range is shrinking. Then Δf is positive when the emitter is closing: the tracked tone sits higher than its at-rest tone. A falling tone, below the rest tone, means opening. A steady approach at constant speed is a plateau, not a ramp. The tone steps up and stays up until the radial speed changes.

Worked numbers, one-way, purely radial, c = 3.00×108 m/s. These are arithmetic, not measurements.

Radial speed2.4 GHz5.8 GHzWhat it feels like
1.5 m/s12 Hz29 HzA walk. Easy to lose in clock drift.
5 m/s40 Hz97 HzSlow multirotor, or a bike.
10 m/s80 Hz193 HzThe headline pair.
15 m/s120 Hz290 HzFast multirotor, still subsonic by a lot.

Check: 2.4×109 × 10 / 3.00×108 = 80 Hz. 5.8×109 × 10 / 3.00×108 = 193 Hz. A more precise c moves these by a fraction of a hertz.

Geometry. Head-on, the radial speed is the full speed, so the shift is as large as this formula gets. A crossing pass puts the velocity sideways at the closest point, so the radial part is about zero and the tone sits on the rest frequency for that instant, then falls as the emitter goes from coming in to going out. Near and loud does not mean a big Doppler shift.

Bulk Doppler and propeller micro-Doppler are different jobs. The bulk shift above is the motion of the whole aircraft, which is the toward/away cue. Spinning blades add a smaller, faster wiggle around that tone. That wiggle is a clue that the emitter has rotors. It does not say the aircraft is approaching.

Passive radar is a neighbor, not this measurement. A passive radar listens to someone else’s illuminator (LTE, DVB, FM) and to the echo off the aircraft. The echo’s Doppler depends on both legs of the path. Listening to the drone’s own transmitter is one-way, and only the aircraft’s radio has to be on. The papers in the third inset live mostly on the echo side. They are here so the two ideas stay distinct.

Where the 80 Hz comes from

The emitter covers vradial meters in one second. The wavelength is λ = c / f, so that distance holds vradial / λ extra crests. Extra crests per second are hertz:

Δf = vradial / λ = f · vradial / c

Same result from range. The phase of the arriving wave changes as the path length changes. The extra frequency is −(f/c) times the range rate. Range rate is negative when the emitter approaches, so the extra frequency is positive.

At 15 m/s, v/c is about 5×10−8. The relativistic correction is of order v2/c2, utterly smaller than an 80 Hz shift on a 2.4 GHz carrier. The first-order formula is the whole story at multirotor speed.

At 2.4 GHz, λ ≈ 12.5 cm. At 5.8 GHz, λ ≈ 5.2 cm. That length shows up again when a second antenna is a baseline for angle.

One-way, a radar echo, and a cellular echo

One-way, this page: the transmitter is on the aircraft, the receiver is on the ground, and the shift is f vradial / c.

Monostatic radar, for contrast: the pulse goes out and the echo comes back, so a target flying straight at the radar produces about twice that shift, 2f v/c. At 2.4 GHz and 10 m/s that is about 160 Hz. We are not building that radar.

Bistatic passive radar: the illuminator and the receiver are in different places. The shift is (f/c) times the sum of the radial speeds along the two legs (illuminator→aircraft and aircraft→receiver). Depending on geometry it lands between about zero and the two-way value. Forward scatter, with the aircraft crossing the line from a satellite to a ground dish, is a case where the bulk shift collapses and the blade wiggle is what remains. That is the DVB-S paper below.

Why 1 ppm is a few kilohertz, and the tone can still be a plateau

A fractional clock error ε moves the apparent carrier by ε · f. One part per million is 10−6.

4 ppm9.6 kHz23 kHzStill much larger
2 ppm4.8 kHz12 kHzStill much larger
1 ppm2.4 kHz5.8 kHzAbout 30× at 2.4 GHz

1 ppb2.4 Hz5.8 HzSmaller than the 10 m/s shift

80 Hz at 2.4 GHz is 0.033 ppm. Reading the sign from an absolute frequency, with no rest reference, wants a clock that stays inside a few hundredths of a ppm for the whole run. That is a GPS-disciplined oscillator or a good ovenized oscillator, not a dongle’s ordinary TCXO.

With a rest reference the bar is kinder. You care how many hertz the tone walks while everyone is sitting still, over the same minute you later spend walking. If that walk is 5 Hz and the approach step is 40 Hz, the sign is visible even though the absolute frequency is kilohertz off. Warm the radio until the rest tone stops marching, then write down the leftover drift. That drift is the noise floor for the sign.

On a constant-speed radial leg the Doppler offset itself is constant, so the slope of the trace is about zero while the aircraft is steadily closing. The sign lives in the height of the plateau above the rest tone. The slope becomes the useful sign on a pass-by: the tone is high on the way in, falls through the closest point, and is low on the way out.

Try another carrier or speed

Same estimate, Δf = f · v / c, with c = 3.00×108 m/s. Positive speed means closing.

Arithmetic only. It assumes a purely radial, one-way path and a perfect clock. It does not know your radio.

What a second channel buys, in one paragraph

Two receivers that share a clock hear the same emitter at f1 and f2. The difference f1 − f2 cancels the shared oscillator and the emitter’s own crystal. What remains is the difference of the two radial speeds, times f/c. The sign of that difference says which antenna the emitter is closing on. Two separate dongles with two free-running clocks leave the difference of their clock errors in that subtraction, and that leftover is usually kilohertz. Coherence, or a GPS-locked reference on each site, is the difference between a demo and a cue you would show an operator.

Open research that touches this

Inset · read these, they are open

Four papers, each kept because the full text was readable and the link is open access. One line on why it matters for toward/away. These are their results, not ours.

  • Bhattacherjee et al., Experimental Study of Outdoor UAV Localization and Tracking using Passive RF Sensing (arXiv:2108.07857). They track a DJI Inspire 2 outdoors by its own 2.4 GHz emission, using time-difference of arrival across four sensors. It is evidence that the aircraft’s own transmitter is a usable outdoor beacon. Their observable is arrival time, not the sign of a frequency shift, so it is the same signal and a different measurement.
  • Ji et al., Doppler-Based Multistatic Drone Tracking via Cellular Downlink Signals (arXiv:2509.25732, HTML v1). Three passive receivers measure bistatic Doppler of LTE echoes off a drone and reconstruct a trajectory from Doppler alone. They report 90% of errors below 0.9 m in that experiment, with the drone about 20 m from the receivers and about 200 m from the base stations. This is the closest cousin: the sign and size of Doppler can carry motion. It is echo Doppler, two-legged, not the one-way shift of the drone’s own radio.
  • Raja Abdullah et al., Passive Forward-Scattering Radar Using a DVB-S Signal for Drone Detection (Remote Sensing 2020, 12(18), 3075). A satellite TV signal, a ground receiver, and the drone crossing that baseline. They pull a blade micro-Doppler feature to say a drone passed. That geometry is the crossing case, where bulk toward/away Doppler collapses and the rotor wiggle does the work.
  • Tang, Ma, Qu, and Mao, UAV Detection with Passive Radar: Algorithms, Applications, and Challenges (Drones 2025, 9(1), 76). An open survey of passive-radar UAV detection: which illuminators people use, and how a range–Doppler map is built from echoes. Read it to see the neighboring field. The survey also separates that echo method from simply listening to the UAV’s own communications, which is the method on this page.

First principles, in the order you would build an opinion

Emitters we might hear

On the ground, the Doppler we can use rides on a transmitter that is moving. That is the aircraft’s own radio: analog video, a digital video downlink, or telemetry. The pilot’s controller is usually sitting still, so a receiver near the pilot hears that uplink with almost no Doppler from the flight.

Classic analog FPV often puts control on 2.4 GHz and a continuous video transmitter on 5.8 GHz. The video carrier is the tempting tone, and it is also wide FM, so the energy is spread over megahertz and an 80 Hz bulk shift can hide inside the deviation. A clean continuous tone is the v0 signal. A hopping digital link (many current camera drones) moves the carrier by far more than the Doppler, dwell by dwell. The Doppler, if it is recoverable at all, is a small residual inside each dwell, not the hop itself.

Control, video, and telemetry, and why hopping is a later problem

Three emissions, three different chances:

  • Uplink, controller to aircraft. The transmitter is on the ground. A ground sensor next to it learns almost nothing about radial speed from that carrier. The aircraft sees the uplink shifted, and we are not on the aircraft.
  • Downlink video or telemetry, aircraft to ground. This is the one-way Doppler on this page. Analog video can be continuous for a whole flight. Digital telemetry may be packets. A packet still has a carrier offset, if the dwell is long enough to measure.
  • Hopping and OFDM. The channel can jump by many megahertz. The residual offset inside a hop is where Doppler and the two crystals live together. Tracking it means either staying inside one dwell or removing the known hop. Dwell times shorter than about a tenth of a second fight a 10 Hz bin. v0 does not take this on. The test rig uses a cooperative continuous tone on purpose.

A drone that is silent (pre-programmed, no radio) gives this method nothing. Passive radar, the other field, still has a chance, because it uses an illuminator the aircraft does not control. That is a different project.

How a measurement would actually work

Tune near the tone. Track the residual, the carrier-frequency offset, over time. Mark a rest segment with the emitter powered and still. The sign of toward/away is the sign of (tone now − rest tone). Positive means closing. The rough speed is that difference plugged back into v ≈ Δf · c / f, and only the radial part comes out.

The slope, the sign of d(Δf)/dt, answers a different question: is the offset rising or falling right now? On a steady radial leg the slope is about zero while the plateau is already telling you the sign. On a pass-by, with no separate rest recording, the falling slope through the closest point is the moment the emitter goes from coming in to going out.

A short coherent baseline is the other way to get a sign without a rest tone: the sign of (frequency at antenna A − frequency at antenna B), with one shared clock.

Rest tone, pass-by, and what to plot

Plot tracked frequency against time, in hertz relative to the tuner, not the absolute carrier. Draw four bands on the plot if the motion is an out-and-back: rest, approach, rest, leave. Approach should sit above the rests. Leave should sit below them. The gap should be larger than the drift you measured inside the rest bands.

A waterfall is the picture. A peak picker or a slow PLL on a narrow tone is the number. Decimate the IQ down to a few tens of kilohertz before the long FFT so a few-hertz bin is cheap. Keep the raw IQ until the plot makes sense.

If the rest tone itself walks by 30 Hz during a one-minute sit, an expected 12 Hz walking shift at 2.4 GHz is not a fair test. Change the speed, the carrier, or the clock until the expected shift, from the table above, is several times the measured rest drift. Compute that expected hertz before anyone moves.

Two antennas

A short baseline answers “which way is the emitter?” A long baseline answers “which site is it closing on?” They are different measurements. v0 uses one antenna.

Short baseline for angle, long baseline for two radials

Short baseline, a fraction of a meter, phase coherence required. The phase difference between two antennas is about 2π (d sin θ) / λ. That θ is a direction of arrival. At 2.4 GHz, λ is 12.5 cm, and a single pair stays unambiguous only while the spacing is under about half a wavelength, around 6 cm. This gives an angle. It does not give toward versus away. Closing and opening can share the same bearing.

Long baseline, tens of meters or more. Each site has its own line of sight, so each has its own radial speed. One site can see a rising tone while the other sees a falling tone, which means the emitter is closing on the first and opening on the second. That is a differential Doppler, not a direction finder. The subtraction is only as good as the shared clock. Two coherent channels on one circuit board give you the short baseline (or as long as the cables). Two radios a hundred meters apart need a GPS-locked reference at each site, or a reference carried between them. Cables that long add loss and their own phase. This is a later experiment, listed under the test rig, not part of v0.

What makes the sign lie

  • Local-oscillator drift. The receiver’s clock walks during the run by more than the Doppler step. A rest segment measures this. If the rest is already wandering through the shift you hope to see, the sign is not yours yet.
  • Multipath. A bounce off a wall or a vehicle arrives with a different radial and a different delay. The peak hops between paths, or two tones beat. v0 wants a straight path in the open for that reason.
  • Hop dwells. The carrier jumps faster than the integration that would resolve 10–100 Hz. The tracker then follows the hop, which is kilohertz to megahertz, and the Doppler is a rounding error.
  • Crossing geometry. At the closest point the radial speed is about zero. A loud, near, fast aircraft can show a rest-like tone for that moment. The trace of a full pass falls through that point; a single snapshot does not.
Two more, smaller, and easy to mistake for Doppler

The aircraft’s own crystal moves with temperature as the flight progresses. A slow thermal walk can look like a gentle opening or closing. The rest test does not catch a drift that only happens once the aircraft is working hard. Keep v0 on a beacon that stays at one temperature, or note the thermal trend on a long sit with the transmitter keyed and still.

A wideband analog video signal has no single-hertz peak. A tracker locked to “the strongest bin” will wander with the modulation. That wander is not radial speed. Prefer a continuous unmodulated tone until the sign is boringly repeatable.

A parked cue for Track 1

The vignette already shows link state: ok, degraded, lost. A parked cue beside that, “this emitter’s tone is up relative to its rest tone,” would mean the aircraft radio is closing on the sensor. Bearing stays a different sensor. The direction lock stays as it is. Active radar stays a different project. direction-lock.md stays untouched.

The cue stays parked. Putting it in the vignette waits until you ask, and until a garage test has shown the sign on a cooperative tone.

Basic test rig

A plan for a garage or a clear lot. No hardware is specified as bought. No result is claimed.

  1. Goal of v0. Show that the sign of the tracked frequency matches a known approach and a known departure, on a cooperative continuous beacon or a narrow tone. Not on a hopping DJI, and not on a wide FM video carrier, until the tone version is dull. Analog video can be a second experiment if you can find a stable residual inside it; it is a poor first proof because the modulation is huge next to 80 Hz.
  2. Parts.
    • One receiver from the table that covers the beacon’s frequency. For a real 2.4 GHz tone that is a Pluto, a HackRF, or a LimeSDR Mini, not a bare RTL-SDR. An RTL-SDR is enough if the legal beacon you already have sits under 1.7 GHz, and you accept a smaller Doppler.
    • An antenna for that band, the one that belongs with the transmitter or a small whip. One antenna for v0.
    • A laptop that can run a waterfall. GNU Radio, CubicSDR, or a short Python capture of the IQ are all enough. No new software in this repo.
    • A continuous-tone source you are allowed to turn on: a lab generator, the transmitter half of a radio you own, or a certified video sender only after the tone test works. A phone GPS is a weak speed sensor at a walk. A tape measure and a stopwatch are the ground truth on a short path.
  3. Do the cabled check first. Key the tone into a dummy load or through an attenuator, at the lowest power that still shows one clean peak, on a band you are allowed to use. Shift the transmitter by about +80 Hz, then about −80 Hz, by the radio’s own tuning. The tracker has to report those signs before anyone walks. This checks the software and the clock. It radiates almost nothing.
  4. Then a straight radial path. Receiver fixed. Emitter starts still (rest), comes straight at the antenna, stops (rest), leaves straight away, stops (rest). First outdoors pass: a walk or a bike over a taped line of a few tens of meters, in a clear lot, only if that transmission is legal there. A 50–200 m path is worth it later if the site is safe and the band allows it. Compute the expected hertz first. A walk at 2.4 GHz is about 12 Hz, which is a harsh first demo. A bike, about 5 m/s and about 40 Hz at 2.4 GHz, is a kinder proxy. Do not use a road, and do not aim this at people as a tracking exercise.
  5. Capture. Record IQ, or a waterfall plus a log of the peak frequency versus time. Mark the segments in the log as they happen: rest, approach, rest, leave. Plot the tracked peak. Write the expected hertz on the plot.
  6. Success, proposed. On at least 5 runs, the approach segment sits above the neighboring rest segments, and the leave segment sits below them, by more than the drift inside those rests. Five is a proposal for you to change. Zero runs exist today. Rough speed, turning hertz back into m/s and comparing with the stopwatch, is a second line on the same plot and is optional for v0.
  7. Safety and the law. Listening to someone else’s signal is receive-only: do not transmit back, do not jam, do not follow a person or an aircraft that is not part of your own test. Your own transmitter stays on a band and a power you are allowed to use; if that is unclear, stop at the cabled check. This note is a cue a supervisor might read. It is not a design for interfering with a link or for harming an aircraft.
Later experiments, after the sign is repeatable
  • Coherent dual channel. Two antennas on one shared clock (a B210, or a KrakenSDR on a band it can tune). Repeat the out-and-back and see the differential frequency carry the sign with no rest tone. This is the product-shaped version of the measurement.
  • Outdoor multirotor, known continuous link. The aircraft carries a continuous beacon you control, or a video transmitter that the cabled test has already shown has a trackable residual. Log GPS speed on the aircraft as ground truth. Still a radial out-and-back, then a deliberate crossing pass to watch the tone fall through zero.
  • Hopping residual. Only with a link whose hop plan you know. Ask whether a residual inside each dwell follows the same sign as the continuous-tone test. If the dwell is too short, write that down as a limit, not as a failure of the physics.
