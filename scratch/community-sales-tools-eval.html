Two valuation methods, one 3.125× gap — pick one and be consistent

At a fixed 20% EBITDA margin, 5× ARR is mathematically always 5 ÷ (8 × 0.20) = 3.125× higher than 8× EBITDA — not a coincidence, just what the two multiples imply at this margin. This page uses 8× EBITDA here (a profit multiple, more typical for a steady, moderate-growth profitable business) but 5× ARR elsewhere for today's valuation (a revenue multiple, more typical for a growth-oriented SaaS business being valued on trajectory rather than current profit). Both are defensible conventions individually, but using whichever one is more flattering in each context — instead of applying the same method throughout — isn't. Worth deciding which basis actually fits this company's growth profile and using it consistently across both the Current Business Snapshot and this model.

Structural recommendations

Normalized EBITDA: contractually base profit distributions on normalized EBITDA, to protect against unbudgeted founder expense adjustments.

ARR step-ups: automatic base-salary increases (e.g. +$20,000 per $1M in ARR milestone reached).

Additional Terms: Protecting the CTO's Downside

This offer asks the CTO to take a discounted cash base and illiquid equity in a company with no VC-style exit path or oversight (no board, no other investors watching the founder's conduct). The terms below are standard practice for de-risking exactly that position — not independently sourced to a specific legal reference, and not legal advice. A real employment/equity attorney should draft and review the actual agreement.

Compensation & EBITDA integrity

Written normalization methodology: the "Normalized EBITDA" adjustments above should be defined in writing at signing (what add-backs are and aren't allowed), not left to the founder's discretion after the fact.

Audit right: the CTO can request an independent CPA review of the EBITDA calculation (at company expense) if the profit-share payout is disputed, with a defined resolution process if the audit finds a material discrepancy.

Base salary floor: base cash cannot be unilaterally reduced once set; a below-market entry salary is a one-time negotiated term, not something re-negotiable downward at the founder's option.

Payment cadence: profit-share paid on a defined schedule (e.g. quarterly) rather than at the founder's discretion, with interest accruing on late payments past a grace period.

Equity protection

Anti-dilution floor: the CTO's equity percentage can't be diluted below an agreed floor by new issuances without their consent, outside of a normal future financing round.

Buyback / put right: since this company has no planned VC-style exit, the CTO should have the right (not obligation) to require the company to repurchase vested shares at independently-appraised fair market value starting at a fixed point (e.g. year 5) — otherwise "illiquid equity" can mean equity that's never actually worth anything to them.

Full acceleration on change of control: 100% acceleration (not just double-trigger) if the company is sold — a sale is the one scenario where the founder's incentive to delay vesting and the CTO's interests diverge most sharply.

Right of first refusal protection: any company ROFRDefinitionRight of First Refusal — a clause giving the company the option to buy back the CTO's shares (e.g. before they're sold to a third party) at a set price or method. Protective for the CTO only if that price is pegged to fair market value; if the founder can set the price unilaterally, it's a way to squeeze the CTO out cheaply. on the CTO's shares should be at fair market value, not a discretionary formula the founder controls.

Termination & job security

Severance minimum: a defined severance (e.g. 6 months of base + prorated profit-share) if terminated without cause, so the discounted cash base isn't a bet with zero downside protection.

Narrow "cause" definition: "cause" should be tightly and specifically defined in the contract (e.g. fraud, gross negligence) — not a vague catch-all a founder could invoke to avoid severance or accelerate a buyback at a bad price.

Narrow clawback scope: any clawback of already-paid profit-share or vested equity should be limited to fraud or willful misconduct, never "for any reason" or performance-based after the fact.

Information rights & scope protection

Financial transparency: the right to see full company financials at least quarterly — hard to trust an EBITDA-linked profit share without visibility into the underlying numbers.

Title & authority protection: can't be silently stripped of the CTO title, technical decision authority, or direct reports without cause and without triggering the severance terms above.

Narrow IP assignment: IP assignment limited to work product related to company business, with an explicit carve-out for pre-existing IP and unrelated side projects.

D&O insurance / indemnification: standard but easy to skip in a bootstrapped company — the CTO should be covered by Directors & Officers insurance and indemnified for actions taken in the role.

Is 15% equity skewed toward founder-level? — resolved, using the one verified source

The Art of CTO's real, sourced numbers show non-founder CTO equity declining by stage: 1–2% at Series A, 0.5–1% at Series B–C, 0.2–0.5% at Series D+. That's a materially different framing than the earlier 3% default: at 15%, this reads less like "a discounted-cash-base trade-off" and more like founder-level equity — worth being explicit about which one this offer is actually meant to be, since the structural protections elsewhere on this page (buyback rights, full acceleration, etc.) were written for a non-founder hire, not a co-founder-equivalent stake.
