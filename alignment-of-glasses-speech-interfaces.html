Alignment of Glasses Speech Interfaces

How Apple, Android, Meta and Snap each expect a head-worn app to listen — the interaction timelines, the platform constraints, and the certification clauses that decide whether it ships.

Perishable report Compiled 2026-09-25

Head-mounted speech guidance is thinner than mobile speech guidance on every one of these four platforms. Where a vendor publishes no HMD-specific rule, this report falls back to the comparable phone OS and says so in the cell. Claims carry one of two markers: doc means a named vendor page states it, est. means it is inferred from the mobile equivalent, from a deprecation notice, or from developer reports rather than from a published requirement.

Part 1

Interaction timelines

Two sequences per platform: a discrete command the user fires once, and a continuous listening state the user switches on. Each diagram splits the User, the Hardware/OS, and the App/Engine so you can see exactly which layer owns the microphone indicator and which layer ends the session.

Each tab has one horizontal scrollbar shared by both of its diagrams, so they move together — they are drawn at full width rather than squeezed to fit. Solid arrows into the “what the user sees” lane are changes to the display; dashed arrows back to the User are what they hear. Both lanes are written as the user would describe them rather than as widgets to build. Read the Note bands as constraints rather than steps — they mark the point where the platform, not the app, decides what happens. The recurring pattern across all four: the app owns activation and feedback, the OS owns the indicator, and nobody lets a third-party app own the wake word.

Scope: the app stays in front of the user. Every timeline below runs with the app in the foreground and the user attending to it. Sending it behind something else changes continuous speech on all four platforms — capture can be cut mid-sentence, the session has to be resumed rather than restarted, and a partial transcript has to survive the gap. That recovery is real work and it is not drawn here. The background rules themselves are in Table B.

The state model both channels carry

Every state below changes something the user can see and something the user can hear. Both columns are written as the user would describe them, not as components to build — a meter that tracks amplitude is one way to do the listening row, but what the row actually asks for is that the person can tell the app is live rather than frozen. No platform requires both channels, and no two platforms name the same one, so designing to the mandate alone produces an app that feels different on each device. Design the pair, then drop whichever channel a given device cannot carry.

StateWhat the user seesWhat the user hears
idleNothing new. The app looks the way it did before they thought about speaking.Nothing.
armedWhatever they just acted on changes, and the app names what it is listening for.A short tone as the microphone opens.
listeningSomething responds to the room, so they can tell the app is live rather than frozen or crashed.Nothing. The quiet is what tells them it is their turn.
hearingTheir own words appear as they say them, and look unfinished, so they know the app may still change its mind.Nothing. The app does not speak over the person speaking to it.
interpretingThe words stop changing and settle. The app shows that it is working on them.A short tone as the microphone closes, so they know they can stop talking.
confirmedThe thing the words acted on is pointed out before it changes, so they can see what moved and what did not.The app says back what it did, in one short sentence.
mutedThe app says it is not listening, and says who stopped it — them, or the system.The same closing tone as before. Never the sound that means it worked.
failedThe app says what went wrong in plain words, with the way to try again next to it.A sound clearly unlike the successful one, and the reason spoken aloud if the user has no display to read.

Two of these are easy to skip and cost the most when they are missing. listening has to look different from idle even though nothing has been said yet — otherwise the user cannot tell a live app from a hung one, and starts repeating themselves. muted has to be unmistakable from confirmed even though both end with the microphone closed — otherwise they walk away believing something happened.

Which channel each vendor actually mandates

PlatformThe channel the vendor namesWhat that leaves you
AppleEither. 2.5.14 asks for “a clear visual and/or audible indication when recording”.docBoth are individually optional, so review pressure alone will not make you build the pair. The OS indicator satisfies nothing on its own — it reports capture, not app state.
AndroidVisual, and the OS draws it for you: the mic indicator plus the foreground-service notification.docNo audible requirement at all. Every earcon is yours, which means nothing stops the app shipping silent — and a glanceable device is exactly where silence hurts.
MetaAudible. “Ensure transparent activation” through earcons or conversational cues.docThe visual state is entirely your design. On Ray-Ban Meta there is no display, so the audible channel is the only one there is.
SnapVisual. Published lenses must “display visuals indicating required actions upon activation”.docAudio playback during mic capture is prohibited, so the audible channel is structurally unavailable until the mic closes. Every state change up to interpreting has to be carried visually.doc
The trap. Meta names the audible cue, Snap forbids it while the mic is open, Android mandates neither, and Apple accepts one or the other. Build the pair once as a state machine, then let each platform suppress the channel it cannot carry — rather than designing four different feedback models and discovering at review that three of them are silent.

visionOS 26 inherits the iOS Speech framework wholesale. SpeechAnalyzer and SpeechTranscriber ship on iOS, iPadOS, macOS, visionOS and tvOS 26 and run on-device; SFSpeechRecognizer remains the legacy path. There is no visionOS-only speech API, so the mobile rules are the rules.

Part 2 · Reading

Where the platforms actually diverge

Four things separate these platforms, and none of them is the recognition quality.

Who ends the session. Apple lets you hold the mic for as long as you hold the foreground. Android ends every utterance for you and makes you restart. Meta ends it at twenty seconds whether you like it or not. Snap ends fragments, not sessions. Build the session loop for the tightest one and the others are free.
Where the audio goes. Apple's SpeechAnalyzer is on-device. Android's on-device recognizer is on-device, and Gemini is not. Meta's Voice SDK routes to Wit.ai. Snap's AsrModule returns a NoInternet error, which tells you where it runs. Egress is the difference between one consent line and a privacy-policy clause.
What the user is told, and on which channel. Apple and Android draw the capture indicator for you and make it unsuppressible — but an indicator reports the microphone, not the app’s state, and satisfies neither user. Beyond it the vendors split: Meta names the earcon, Snap names the visual and forbids audio while the mic is open, Android names neither. The only design that survives all four carries every state on both channels and lets the platform mute the one it cannot support. See the state model.
What you are allowed to do with the transcript. This is Snap's sharpest edge — sensitive user data and external connectivity cannot be combined in a published lens, so the transcript cannot simply leave for your own backend. Apple's 5.1.2 (i) permits it with explicit disclosed permission. Meta wants the justification at review. Android wants it on the Data safety form.
