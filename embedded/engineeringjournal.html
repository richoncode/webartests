Tue 15 Sep 2026 · 16:10 PDT The chance model reaches the panelflashed A launch that scored 10% this morning was on the glass at 89% by evening. Getting there turned up four bugs the panel had been hiding, one of which fired on every flash.

The model, and how it was checked

chance.h touches neither the network nor the clock, which is the whole reason it can be trusted: tools/chance-test.cpp compiles it with a host compiler and asserts every row against what chance-model.py — the model in the plan — returns for identical inputs.

18:47 Friday, Go                     29%  ok
the same, 40% cloud                  21%  ok

The first run failed three of those rows. The code was right and the table was stale — I had copied the expected values from the journal's worked examples, which used a sunset four minutes earlier. Expectations now come from running the model itself, so the test compares two implementations rather than an implementation against my typing.

The floor is 5%, not 15%. A launch at ten percent is still a launch happening tonight, and "probably not, but it is up there at 6:29" is what the band is for. Every launch of the day is scored and the best one wins, rather than the first to clear a threshold.

The first two were silent. A layout change that does not repaint looks exactly like a flash that failed, and a device that boots into a synthetic test frame looks the same again — both of which happened repeatedly before either was understood. LAYOUT_VERSION now mixes into the hash, and a button is only believed when esp_reset_reason() says the chip actually woke from sleep; a cold boot clears test mode and repaints, so a fresh flash always shows what that build draws.

A refused request erased the launch. The panel said "no launch tonight" when the truth was "I could not ask" — the worst kind of wrong, because it is indistinguishable from a correct answer. On its own schedule the device wakes twice a day and would never meet this limit; it surfaced because the button was pressed repeatedly and because I was querying the same endpoint from a laptop while testing.

The answer now survives a refusal. The launch is held in RTC memory — name, chance, time, prep, drift, booster — keyed to the day and the lift-off minute, and shown when the network will not talk. It expires by itself once the rocket has gone, so it cannot report a launch that already happened. And because the booster and the orbit do not change between wakes, a launch already asked about is not asked about again: one request per wake instead of two.

The throttle also showed what absent data defaults to. With the detail request refused, the booster read expended for a mission that lands on a droneship — a missing field reading as a definite claim rather than as unknown.

The band, as it now reads

FALCON 9 - USSF-259                        89%    7:18 PM
LOOK SSE - out by 7:13 - booster: droneship      chance tonight

Three booster outcomes rather than two, because a droneship landing is still a recovery — it simply is not a second thing to watch from here. Only an expended booster is no return at all. The line is assembled in the renderer, where it can be measured, and drops its parts in order when it will not fit: the drift hint first, then the look label, then the prep time. The booster is last, because it is the part worth walking outside for.

The drift hint comes from the mission's orbit and the geometry of the two sites. SLC-4E bears 162° from San Martin at 178 miles; a polar or sun-synchronous flight leaves the pad on an azimuth of 180 to 189, which is 18 to 27 degrees right of that line, so it drifts right and recedes down the coast. Low-inclination flights lean the other way, but Launch Library publishes the orbit and not the azimuth, and LEO spans inclinations that go both ways — so that case says only that the track is shallower.

The forecast columns, and the light

Sky and chance of rain read as one fact, so they share a line. The height that frees goes into the temperatures: high from F_MID to F_BIG, low from F_BODY to F_MID, measured at 67 + 7 + 51 px against a 156 px column before it was committed rather than after.

The sources moved to a strip of their own along the bottom edge, one line, and the footer gave up exactly that height so nothing above it moved.

The LED now blinks from the moment a button wakes the device, not from the moment the panel moves. Wi-Fi and three fetches stand between the press and the first pixel, and without it the thing looks ignored for the best part of ten seconds. A press that finds nothing new to draw gets two deliberate flashes instead of silence: heard, worked, nothing changed. Every path to sleep puts the light out.

Still ahead

  • The quote of the day, the water-filter badge and the permanent band are mockup and plan; the firmware still hides the band when no launch qualifies.
  • The firmware calls Launch Library v2.2.0, which is deprecated. It answers today. When it stops, the band will disappear with no other symptom — the same failure the cache now covers for a throttle.

Tue 15 Sep 2026 · 11:20 PDT A line for the days without a launch77 quotes, measured The band goes empty on most days. Give it a quotation instead — classical, liberal, empowering — one a day, and prove every one of them fits before a panel ever sees it.

The band, always occupied

Most days carry no launch worth standing outside for, and the band simply vanishes. A line of the day fills it: a launch when there is something to see, a quotation otherwise, and the row never disappears.

The day of the year picks the line, so it is fixed for the whole day and the panel repaints once when it turns over — the same discipline as every other field.

Choosing them

The brief was classical yet liberal, and empowering. That reads as the Stoics and the Greeks for self-command, Rome for nerve, the Enlightenment and the abolitionists for liberty, and a few later voices who earned a place in the same argument. Nothing devotional, nothing partisan, and nothing that reads like a motivational poster.

77 lines · 51 distinct voices
Seneca ×4 · Marcus Aurelius ×3 · Epictetus ×3 · Frederick Douglass ×3

Attribution is the hard part. A great many quotations circulate under the wrong name, so where the popular attribution is wrong the real author is credited: E. B. Hall, not Voltaire, for defending to the death the right to say it; John Philpot Curran, not Jefferson, for eternal vigilance; Ian Maclaren, not Plato, for everyone fighting a hard battle. Lines whose source could not be established were dropped however well they read — several of the most-quoted Emerson and Twain lines are not theirs.

Proving they fit, without a panel

A quotation that overruns the band is a defect you would only find on the glass, days later, on whichever day that line came round. So the widths are measured here instead.

tools/gfxfont.py parses the GFX font headers and sums each glyph's xAdvance — the same arithmetic Adafruit GFX performs on the device — so the number it returns is the number the panel will produce. tools/check-quotes.py runs every entry through it against the band's 756 px of usable width:

77 quotes · 71 fit at F_BODY · 6 need F_TINY · 0 do not fit

The check exits non-zero when one does not fit, so a quotation added later cannot quietly overrun. The quote is drawn at F_BODY where it fits and F_TINY where it does not, the attribution always F_TINY and right-aligned.

What this costs the rest of the panel

The band stops being optional. computeLayout currently hands its 56 pixels and a rule back to the rows above when there is no launch, so those rows have been laid out with the extra space available. Making the band permanent takes it away on every day, and the forecast, event and bin rows have to be checked in the mockup at their new heights before this goes anywhere near the device.

But the same rule hides every marginal evening and every pre-dawn launch, which is exactly when a heads-up would earn its place.

Why the panel is blank tonight

The weather passed and the clock failed. That is the rule working: what makes one of these worth walking outside for at 200 miles is the twilight plume — the rocket climbing into sunlight while you are already standing in the dark — and an hour before sunset there is no plume to see.

The trouble is what else that rule hides. It is a threshold, and a launch at 18:47 with sunset at 19:09 fails it by the same silence as one at noon. It also has no idea that dawn exists: USSF R-3 on the 27th lifts at 04:42, two hours before sunrise, into exactly the same twilight geometry from the other side, and the current code would never mention it.

The replacement: a curve and a number

Score the opportunity instead of ruling on it, and put the score on the panel:

chance = twilight(t) × sky(cloud) × confidence(status)
TermWhat it readsShape

What can be proved without the device

  1. The scoring, on the host. chance.h takes minutes and percentages and returns a number, so g++ compiles it against a table of cases and asserts every row — the seven above become the test.
  2. The layout, headless. The mockup render check already sweeps the control combinations; the new states go into that sweep. Nothing about the band's geometry needs a panel to verify.
  3. The build. arduino-cli compile needs no board attached.
  4. The live data. The LL2 and Open-Meteo queries can be run from here against the real endpoints and scored, which is how the table above was produced.

What cannot: the ink. Whether yellow text-on-band reads at arm's length, and whether a 26 px percentage is legible after a full refresh, are panel questions.

Open decisions

  • The deep-night floor. A clear midnight launch scores 13% and stays hidden. At 200 miles with no illuminated plume there is genuinely little to see — but a bright first stage and a staging flash are not nothing. Raising the floor to 0.2 would put those on the panel.
  • Tomorrow's pre-dawn. The filter is "today". An evening refresh cannot warn about a 04:42 launch the next morning, because the day boundary falls between them. Extending to "the next 18 hours" would fix it and costs one more day of hourly cloud.
  • Trajectory. Every Vandenberg launch here is treated as heading SSE. Polar and sun-synchronous flights differ enough to matter, and LL2 carries the orbit abbreviation in the same response that is already being fetched.

48 × 48 × 33 mm, 50 g, in aluminium alloy, plastic and acrylic. The module is a finished enclosure — nothing needs to wrap it, and reprinting it would be a downgrade in materials.

The STEP file and the published figures disagree. The model's geometry spans 47.3 × 43.1 × 35.0 mm across its points, against a published 48 × 48 × 33. Some of that is expected — spline control points bound a surface rather than sit on it, so a point cloud over-measures — but 43.1 against 48 is too large a gap to be that. One of the two is wrong and neither can be trusted to half a millimetre, which is exactly the tolerance a press fit needs.

There is no charger on this board

The schematic answers the question the wiki does not: no charger IC, and no battery net anywhere in it. U2 is an RY3420 step-down regulator, so the ZX-MX 1.25-4P port marked "charging" is a 5 V input, not a cell connector.

A single lithium cell sits at 3.0–4.2 V, below what a step-down needs to make 5 V, so a battery has to come through a boost converter. Every milliamp on the 5 V rail then costs about 1.53 mA out of the cell — 5 V from 3.7 V at 88% — and the boost converter's own quiescent draw lands on top of the sleep figure. A 2000 mAh cell at 2 mA rail sleep is 11 days, not the 17 that a cell wired straight to the rail would have given.

The parts that follow from that: a protected cell, a TP4056 charge module, and a boost with a low quiescent current. Three boards where one would have done if the port had been what its name suggests.

Deep sleep cannot see the knob

Only GPIO 0–21 are RTC-capable on the ESP32-S3. A device you can only wake by touching the glass is the wrong device when the knob is the point.

The wake level is chosen per pin from its level at the moment of sleeping, so whichever way the encoder happens to be resting, a turn changes something and that change is the wake.

Two things happen before the radio goes: the bulb sockets are dropped deliberately rather than discovered dead on the next write, and sleep is refused while a send is in flight — a half-written frame leaves a bulb's single connection slot held open.

What the class offers

EPaper subclasses TFT_eSprite, so all drawing lands in an in-RAM framebuffer and only a handful of calls reach the panel:

CallWhat it does
fillScreen() / fillSprite()writes the RAM buffer; no panel activity at all
update()pushes the whole buffer and runs the waveform
update(x,y,w,h,data)pushes a region from a caller-supplied buffer
updataPartial(x,y,w,h)the library's partial-refresh path (spelling is theirs)

Clearing is therefore always two steps — fill the buffer, then update(). Nothing skips the waveform.

Three reasons a region refresh cannot work here

  • The partial path does not exist for this driver. Combo 521 selects ED2208_DRIVER, and updataPartial() ends by calling EPD_UPDATE_PARTIAL(). That macro appears zero times in ED2208_Defines.h, with no #ifndef fallback anywhere in the library — the call would not compile. Six drivers do define it, all mono or black/white/red/yellow. None is Spectra 6.
  • There is no window to set. EPD_SET_WINDOW on ED2208 is do { } while (0). Even the full-frame path is not windowing anything; the macro survives for drivers that support it.
  • The data would land in the wrong place. EPD_PUSH_NEW_COLORS issues command 0x10 and streams w/2 × h bytes into panel RAM from offset zero. Without a window a 200×100 region would overwrite the top-left corner rather than appear at (x,y). The x and y arguments only reach pushImage into the local sprite buffer; they never leave the microcontroller.

The refresh cost is not data transfer. EPD_UPDATE() is a bare 0x12 with no region parameter, so it drives the whole panel through the Spectra colour passes however many bytes were sent:

frame buffer              192,000 bytes at 4 bpp
update() pushes OLD+NEW   384,000 bytes

  transfer at 20 MHz        0.15 s    0.4%
  waveform                 37.7 s   99.6%

The inversion

esptool uploads a stub flasher by default because it moves data roughly twice as fast as the ROM loader. On this link that advantage disappears. The failure is not a clean threshold. A single 256 kB read at 115200 with the stub passed on the first attempt, which is what made 115200 look reliable at first; repeating it showed 2 of 5. One sample was not enough to characterise a flaky link.

The pair 1A86:7523 is the fixed identifier of the WCH CH340 USB-to-UART bridge — see DeviceHunt and the usb.ids registry. A CH340 is a bare bridge chip: it reports no board identity, only the generic string USB Serial, so the descriptors alone cannot name what sits behind it.

The bridge chip does not narrow the field much on its own. Seeed's XIAO family presents native USB under Seeed's own vendor ID 0x2886 — the Seeeduino XIAO enumerates as 2886:802F, per Seeed's driver INF — but many boards, Seeed's own included, route USB through a third-party UART bridge instead. A CH340 is equally consistent with a USB-to-TTL adapter, an Arduino clone, or a controller board.

Method

pyserial is absent from the Homebrew Python, so the probe drives the port through raw termios instead of adding a dependency. Three choices keep the probe non-destructive:

  • Open the callout node /dev/cu.*, which does not block waiting for carrier detect.
  • Clear HUPCL in the control flags, so closing the port does not drop DTR and reset an attached board.
  • Send only read-only queries: CR/LF, GRBL's ? status request and $I build-info request, and Marlin's M115 firmware-info request.

A second pass adds an Arduino-style auto-reset — assert DTR and RTS for 200 ms, release, then listen — and reaches non-standard baud rates through the macOS IOSSIOSPEED ioctl (0x80045402), which termios speed constants do not cover.

Reading the result

Three bursts totalling 36 bytes arrived across roughly thirty trials, at three unrelated baud rates, and none reproduced on a second attempt at the same rate. A genuine transmitter would produce framing errors at every wrong rate and clean text at the right one; it would also repeat. A floating UART RX pin picks up noise and emits exactly this pattern — occasional short random bursts, unrepeatable.

Conclusion: the CH340 bridge and its macOS driver work — the port opens, accepts baud changes, and drives DTR/RTS. Nothing is talking on the UART behind it.

Candidate causes

  • The adapter is a bare USB-to-TTL cable with nothing wired to its RX/TX pins.
  • The attached board is unpowered, held in reset, or runs firmware that prints nothing on boot.
  • TX is wired to TX and RX to RX rather than crossed.
  • Logic levels mismatch — a 5 V adapter against a 3.3 V board, or the reverse.
