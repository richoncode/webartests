GNSS & RTK under the hood

How a receiver pulls a signal buried ~20 dB under thermal noise out of the air, turns it into ranges, and then into centimetres, plus the protocols that carry corrections and the ways all of it gets jammed, reflected and spoofed. No products, no vendors: signals, protocols and math, with links to the primary documents.

0The 30-second model

Every satellite transmits a carrier modulated by a known pseudorandom spreading code and a slow data message. The receiver makes a local replica of each code, slides it in time and frequency until it correlates, and reads off when the code it is hearing was transmitted, according to the satellite's clock. Multiply the apparent travel time by \(c\) and you get a pseudorange. It is "pseudo" because the receiver clock is wrong by an unknown bias \(b\). With four or more satellites you solve for \((x,y,z,b)\). Everything else in this page is about doing that with less noise and fewer biases, from metres down to millimetres, and about doing it when someone or something is trying to stop you.

Two measurements matter. Code phase is unambiguous but noisy (decimetres to metres). Carrier phase is about 100× finer (millimetres) but ambiguous by an unknown whole number of ~19 cm wavelengths. RTK is, at heart, the business of finding those integers.

1Signal structure & CDMA

1.1 Carriers and why they live where they do

All core GNSS signals sit in L-band, between about 1.16 and 1.61 GHz. That's a compromise. Ionospheric delay scales as \(1/f^2\), so lower frequencies hurt, while rain and free-space loss push the other way. The allocations are also protected as radionavigation-satellite bands (ARNS/RNSS). Every civil GPS carrier is an integer multiple of the 10.23 MHz satellite reference: \(L1 = 154 f_0\), \(L2 = 120 f_0\), \(L5 = 115 f_0\).

1.2 Direct-sequence spreading in one equation

A single CDMA component as received, after downconversion to an IF \(f_{IF}\), is:

\[ s_k(t) = \sqrt{2C_k}\; d_k(t-\tau_k)\, c_k(t-\tau_k)\, \mathrm{sc}(t-\tau_k)\, \cos\!\big(2\pi (f_{IF}+f_{D,k}) t + \theta_k\big) + n(t) \]

Here \(c_k\) is the ±1 spreading code, \(d_k\) the data (or 1 on a pilot), \(\mathrm{sc}\) an optional square-wave subcarrier (BOC), \(f_{D,k}\) the Doppler (up to about ±5 kHz for a static receiver, from satellite motion of ~3.9 km/s), and \(\tau_k\) the delay you actually want. Correlating against a replica over \(T\) seconds gives

\[ I + jQ \;\approx\; \sqrt{C_k}\,T\; d\; R_k(\Delta\tau)\; \mathrm{sinc}(\pi \Delta f\, T)\; e^{j\Delta\theta} \;+\; \text{noise}, \]

so you get a peak shaped by the code autocorrelation \(R(\Delta\tau)\) and a sinc in frequency error. That's the whole search problem: find the \((\Delta\tau,\Delta f)\) where \(|I+jQ|\) peaks.

1.3 BPSK vs BOC vs MBOC

Multiplying the code by a square-wave subcarrier splits the spectrum into two lobes offset by \(\pm m \times 1.023\) MHz. This does two useful things. It moves energy away from band centre, which was crowded with legacy C/A and P(Y). And it increases the Gabor bandwidth (RMS bandwidth), which is what sets ranging precision:

\[ \sigma_\tau \;\propto\; \frac{1}{\beta_{rms}\sqrt{2\,T\,C/N_0}}, \qquad \beta_{rms}^2 = \frac{\int f^2 G(f)\,df}{\int G(f)\,df}. \]

MBOC is the US–EU agreed spectral shape for the common L1/E1 open signal: \(G_{MBOC}(f) = \tfrac{10}{11}G_{BOC(1,1)}(f) + \tfrac{1}{11}G_{BOC(6,1)}(f)\). That small BOC(6,1) share adds high-frequency energy for wideband receivers. Narrowband receivers can ignore it and treat the signal as BOC(1,1). It is implemented differently by each system, but they share the same spectrum:

  • Galileo E1, CBOC(6,1,1/11): a weighted sum of BOC(1,1) and BOC(6,1) subcarriers on every chip, with opposite sign on data vs pilot.
  • GPS L1C, TMBOC(6,1,4/33): time-multiplexed. On the pilot, 4 of every 33 chips use BOC(6,1), the rest BOC(1,1), while the data channel is pure BOC(1,1). The pilot gets 75% of the power, and the arithmetic works out to the same 1/11 BOC(6,1) share overall.
  • BeiDou B1C, QMBOC(6,1,4/33): BOC(1,1) and BOC(6,1) on the pilot, placed in quadrature.

That shared shape is what interoperability means at the physical layer. One RF front-end and one correlator architecture handle GPS L1C, Galileo E1 and BeiDou B1C, and their spectral overlap was negotiated so that each adds a bounded amount of interference to the others.

1.4 Spreading codes: Gold, Weil, memory, and the length tradeoff

Gold codes (C/A). Each C/A code is the XOR of two 10-stage maximal-length LFSR sequences, \(G1 = 1 + x^3 + x^{10}\) and \(G2 = 1+x^2+x^3+x^6+x^8+x^9+x^{10}\), with a PRN-specific delay of G2 (set by tap selection). Length is \(2^{10}-1 = 1023\). Gold-family cross-correlation is three-valued, \(\{-1, -65, 63\}/1023\), so the worst case sits at about \(20\log_{10}(65/1023) \approx -23.9\) dB below the autocorrelation peak. That ~24 dB is the C/A code's entire "multiple-access isolation" budget, and it is not much (see near-far, below).

Longer codes. L5, L1C, E5 and B1C/B2a use 10230-chip codes (Galileo E1 uses 4092). Longer codes push cross-correlation sidelobes down roughly as \(1/\sqrt{N}\) in the random-code limit, so about −40 dB for 10230. The cost is a bigger acquisition search. Tiered codes (a short secondary or "overlay" code modulating whole primary periods: Neuman-Hofman codes on L5, 25-chip on E1-C, the 1800-bit L1C overlay) extend the effective period to tens of ms or seconds. That lowers cross-correlation further and helps symbol synchronisation, without making the first acquisition stage any longer.

Weil codes (L1C, B1C) are built from Legendre sequences of prime length (10223 for L1C, padded to 10230 with a fixed 7-chip insertion). They have good, provable correlation bounds. Memory codes (Galileo E1, E6) are not generated by any compact algorithm. They were optimised offline (e.g. for low autocorrelation sidelobes and cross-correlation and for spectral balance), and receivers store them in ROM as a table: ~4 kbit per PRN for E1.

def ca_code(prn_taps):           # e.g. PRN 1 -> (2, 6)
    g1 = [1]*10; g2 = [1]*10; out = []
    for _ in range(1023):
        g2i = g2[prn_taps[0]-1] ^ g2[prn_taps[1]-1]
        out.append(g1[9] ^ g2i)
        g1 = [g1[2] ^ g1[9]] + g1[:9]                       # 1 + x^3 + x^10
        g2 = [g2[1]^g2[2]^g2[5]^g2[7]^g2[8]^g2[9]] + g2[:9] # 1+x^2+x^3+x^6+x^8+x^9+x^10

1.5 Data vs pilot

A data channel's bit transitions (every 20 ms on C/A) cap coherent integration and force a Costas-type phase detector, which is insensitive to 180° flips and so has a narrower pull-in range and a worse threshold. A pilot channel carries no data, only an overlay code you know in advance. That allows long coherent integration (100 ms or more), a pure PLL with a full ±180° discriminator, and roughly 3 dB better tracking threshold than a Costas loop (≈, depends on loop design). Modern signals split power between the two: 50/50 on L5, E1 and E5, and 75% pilot on L1C.

1.6 GLONASS FDMA

GLONASS legacy signals give every satellite the same 511-chip code but a different carrier, at channel \(k\) spaced 562.5 kHz on L1. Antipodal satellites share a channel number, which is why there are only 14 channels for 24 slots. FDMA gives good inter-satellite isolation without code tricks, and narrowband interference only hits one or two channels. The price is inter-frequency biases: each channel takes a slightly different group delay through the receiver front-end. In RTK between dissimilar receivers, those differential biases don't cancel cleanly, which complicates GLONASS ambiguity fixing (RTCM even has a message, 1230, for code-phase biases). Newer GLONASS satellites add CDMA signals (L3OC, at 1202.025 MHz) for this reason.

1.7 The link budget: working below the noise floor

IS-GPS-200 specifies a minimum received L1 C/A power of −158.5 dBW = −128.5 dBm, into a 3 dBi linearly polarised antenna at ≥5° elevation. Actual received power is usually a few dB higher. Now compare that with thermal noise:

\[ N_0 = kT_0 = -174\ \text{dBm/Hz}\ (T_0=290\,\text{K}); \quad N = N_0 + 10\log_{10}(2.046\times10^6) \approx -110.9\ \text{dBm in the C/A main lobe}. \]
Worked example: C/A link budget Signal power −128.5 dBm vs noise −110.9 dBm in 2.046 MHz gives −17.6 dB. Add a 2 dB system noise figure and the pre-correlation SNR ≈ −19.6 dB, so the signal really is buried. In density terms: \[ C/N_0 = -128.5 - (-174 + 2) = 43.5\ \text{dB-Hz}. \] Correlate coherently for \(T = 1\) ms (one code period): post-correlation SNR \(= C/N_0 + 10\log_{10}T = 43.5 - 30 = 13.5\) dB. The jump from −19.6 to +13.5 dB, ≈ 33 dB, is the processing gain \(\approx 10\log_{10}(B\,T) = 10\log_{10}(2.046\times10^6 \times 10^{-3})\). Extend to \(T = 20\) ms (one data bit) and you get \(\approx 26.5\) dB. Open-sky C/N0 is typically ≈ 40–50 dB-Hz. Indoors it can drop below 20 dB-Hz.

Processing gain is also the anti-jam margin. Any interference not matching the code gets spread by the despreading operation and mostly filtered out (§8).

1.8 Why satellites sharing a band don't collide

All GPS satellites transmit on the same L1 carrier at the same time. They are separated by code orthogonality, which is only approximate. After despreading the wanted PRN, every other signal on the band shows up as a noise-like floor. The standard way to quantify it is the spectral separation coefficient (SSC):

\[ \kappa_{s,i} = \int_{-B/2}^{B/2} G_s(f)\,G_i(f)\,df, \qquad \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\!eff} = \frac{C}{N_0 + \sum_i C_i\,\kappa_{s,i}}. \]

Multiple-access interference (MAI) floor. With N similar signals at similar power, the effective noise floor rises by \(\sim\! N C \kappa\). For today's constellations this costs a fraction of a dB to roughly 1–2 dB of effective C/N0 on L1 (≈). It grows as constellations and power levels grow, which is exactly what the MBOC negotiations budgeted for.

Near-far. SSC assumes similar received powers. If one signal is 20–25 dB stronger (a pseudolite, a strong SBAS GEO, or one satellite in clear view while another is under heavy foliage indoors), C/A's −24 dB cross-correlation spikes from the strong PRN can exceed the true peak of the weak one. The receiver then acquires a ghost at the strong satellite's code phase and Doppler offset by multiples of 1 kHz (because the C/A code repeats every 1 ms). High-sensitivity receivers explicitly check for this: does the candidate's Doppler and code phase line up with a strong PRN already being tracked? Some also cancel strong signals by successive interference cancellation (SIC). Longer codes such as L1C, E1 and L5 shrink the problem by 15 dB or more.

Doppler matters. Cross-correlation is worse at particular Doppler offsets: the residual frequency \(\Delta f\) modulates the code product, and some combinations land near the code's spectral lines. Tables of "worst-case C/A cross-correlation vs Doppler" are a standard tool in high-sensitivity receiver design.

2Navigation messages & communication efficiency

Ranging tells you when a signal left. To turn that into a position you also need to know where the satellite was at that instant and how far off its clock was. That's the job of the navigation message: a slow, very robust broadcast data link.

2.1 Why so slow? A capacity argument

Worked example: how much of the channel GNSS actually uses At C/N0 = 43.5 dB-Hz (§1.7), the low-SNR Shannon capacity is \(C \approx (C/N_0)\log_2 e \approx 22{,}400 \times 1.443 \approx 32\) kbit/s per signal. LNAV uses 50 bit/s, about 0.15% of that. The point is the weak end. At 50 bps, \(E_b/N_0 = C/N_0 - 10\log_{10}(50) = C/N_0 - 17\) dB. Uncoded BPSK needs \(E_b/N_0 \approx 9.6\) dB for a \(10^{-5}\) bit error rate, so LNAV demodulates down to roughly 27 dB-Hz, and rate-½ convolutional coding (≈ 5 dB coding gain with soft decisions, approximate) pushes that a few dB lower. The data rate is chosen so data demodulation never becomes the limit before tracking does.

2.2 GPS LNAV (L1 C/A, 50 bps)

  • A word is 30 bits: 24 data plus 6 parity, an extended Hamming code that also uses the last two bits of the previous word, so polarity ambiguity from the Costas loop is resolved as a side effect.
  • A subframe is 10 words = 300 bits = 6 s. Word 1 is the TLM, starting with the preamble 10001011. Word 2 is the HOW, carrying a 17-bit truncated time-of-week count in 6 s units.
  • A frame is 5 subframes = 30 s. Subframes 1–3 carry this satellite's clock and ephemeris, repeated every frame. Subframes 4–5 are paged over 25 frames, a 12.5 min superframe, and carry the almanac, the Klobuchar ionosphere coefficients and the UTC parameters.
  • The ephemeris is Keplerian with perturbations: 16 parameters (\(t_{oe}, \sqrt{A}, e, i_0, \Omega_0, \omega, M_0, \Delta n, \dot i, \dot\Omega, C_{uc}, C_{us}, C_{rc}, C_{rs}, C_{ic}, C_{is}\)). It's uploaded roughly every 2 h and fit over a ~4 h interval (nominal).

LNAV is a fixed TDM schedule with no FEC beyond parity and no CRC over the whole subframe. It's simple and robust, but ephemeris takes at least 18 s, and a single lost subframe costs you 30 s.

2.3 CNAV (L2C, L5) and CNAV-2 (L1C)

  • CNAV uses packetised 300-bit messages: an 8-bit preamble, PRN, a 6-bit message type, TOW, an alert flag, payload and a CRC-24Q. They're sent with rate-½, K=7 convolutional coding (25 bps → 50 sps on L2C, 50 bps → 100 sps on L5). Message types (10/11 ephemeris, 30–37 clock plus other data, and so on) can be scheduled flexibly. Ephemeris has higher-resolution fields and a ~3 h fit.
  • CNAV-2 is an 18 s frame of 1800 symbols at 100 sps:
    • Subframe 1 is a 9-bit time-of-interval, BCH(51,8)-encoded to 52 symbols, so it's very robust and fast to decode.
    • Subframe 2 carries clock and ephemeris: 600 bits including CRC, rate-½ LDPC to 1200 symbols.
    • Subframe 3 is variable pages: 274 bits, LDPC to 548 symbols.
    Subframes 2–3 are block-interleaved. Because the full ephemeris and clock repeat every 18 s and LDPC works near capacity, decoding holds up at C/N0 levels where LNAV fails.

2.4 Galileo I/NAV and F/NAV

  • I/NAV (E1-B, E5b-I, 250 sps) is sent in page parts of 1 s, each a 10-symbol sync pattern plus 240 symbols from rate-½ K=7 convolutional coding and 8×30 block interleaving. An even/odd pair makes a 2 s page with a 128-bit data word and CRC-24. There are 15 pages per 30 s subframe and 24 subframes per 720 s frame. E1-B and E5b-I carry the same words in staggered order, so a dual-frequency receiver collects ephemeris about twice as fast. Newer ICD revisions add a reduced CED word (a coarse ephemeris from a single page, for fast first fix) and a Reed-Solomon outer code across the ephemeris words (FEC2).
  • F/NAV (E5a-I, 25 bps → 50 sps) has 10 s pages, 50 s subframes and a 600 s frame. It's optimised for single-frequency E5a users.
  • C/NAV on E6-B runs at 1000 sps and is the vehicle for the High Accuracy Service corrections (§6.5).

GLONASS, for contrast, broadcasts its ephemeris as an ECEF state vector (position, velocity and lunisolar acceleration) every 30 minutes. The receiver numerically integrates the equations of motion (Runge-Kutta) between reference epochs, instead of evaluating a Kepler fit. It uses 2 s strings (85 bits including 8 Hamming check bits, plus a time mark) and a 30 s frame.

2.6 Assisted GNSS: moving bits to a fatter pipe

A-GNSS sends the slow content (ephemeris or long-term predicted orbits, coarse time, approximate position, even expected code phase and Doppler) over a cellular or IP link at kbit/s or more. The benefits:

  • It removes the need to demodulate the satellite data at all, which matters at C/N0 well below the ~27 dB-Hz demodulation threshold.
  • It shrinks the search window by orders of magnitude.
  • It lets the receiver use known data bits for data wipe-off, which allows coherent integration longer than 20 ms.

If the receiver only has coarse time (within a couple of seconds), it can't resolve the full pseudorange from sub-millisecond code phases. Instead it adds time as a fifth unknown and solves a "coarse-time" navigation problem. The control-plane protocols are 3GPP LPP/RRLP and OMA SUPL; at the wire level they're ASN.1-encoded containers for ephemeris and clock fields that mirror the ICDs.

2.7 Authentication in the message: Galileo OSNMA

OSNMA authenticates the I/NAV data using TESLA, a broadcast authentication protocol built on delayed key disclosure:

  1. The ground segment generates a one-way key chain \(K_{i} = \mathrm{trunc}\big(H(K_{i+1}\,\|\,\mathrm{GST}_{i}\,\|\,\alpha)\big)\) and broadcasts the chain in reverse.
  2. Satellites transmit MAC tags over navigation data computed with \(K_i\) before \(K_i\) is disclosed.
  3. When \(K_i\) is disclosed, about a subframe later, the receiver checks it hashes forward to an already-trusted key, then verifies the earlier tags.
  4. The chain's root key is signed with ECDSA (P-256 or P-521). The public key is anchored via a Merkle tree.

Bandwidth: OSNMA takes 40 bits from each 2 s I/NAV page on E1-B (8 bits of "HKROOT" header and root-key material plus 32 bits of MACK tags and keys), i.e. 600 bits per 30 s subframe. The receiver needs a loosely synchronised clock: if it can't bound GST, it can't know a key wasn't disclosed before the tag arrived.

What OSNMA does and does not protectIt proves the data came from Galileo. It does not prove the ranging signal wasn't delayed. A meaconer that records and re-broadcasts real signals passes OSNMA, and a sophisticated spoofer can replay authenticated data with altered timing as long as it stays within the receiver's time-uncertainty budget. That's why OSNMA pairs with timing checks and the signal-level defences in §9.
Deep dive: why TESLA instead of signing every messagecrypto

A P-256 ECDSA signature (about 128-bit security) is 512 bits. Signing each satellite's ephemeris every 30 s on a 125 bps (net) link would eat most of the channel. TESLA amortises one asymmetric signature (the root key, refreshed rarely) over many cheap symmetric MACs, truncated to tens of bits each, because the attacker only gets one real-time attempt per tag. The trade is latency: authentication arrives about a subframe after the data, and it needs time synchronisation. Symmetric tags are also why the time bound matters. Once a key is public, anyone could forge tags for data "from before" the disclosure unless the receiver can tell that the tag arrived too late.

3Receiver signal processing

Chain: antenna, then LNA and filter, then downconversion, then ADC. Typically only 1–4 bits are needed, because the signal is buried in noise; the implementation loss of 2-bit quantisation is ≈ 0.5 dB. After that come per-channel correlators fed by acquisition and tracking loops. Everything below happens in the digital domain on complex baseband samples \(x[n]\).

3.1 Acquisition: a 2-D search

For each PRN, search code phase \(\tau\) and Doppler \(f_D\):

\[ S(\tau, f_D) = \left| \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, c[n-\tau]\, e^{-j2\pi f_D n T_s} \right|^2 . \]
  • Code dimension: 1023 chips, searched at ≤ ½-chip steps, so ~2046 cells for C/A.
  • Doppler dimension: about ±5 kHz from satellite motion plus receiver motion and oscillator error (a 1 ppm TCXO is ±1.575 kHz at L1). The bin width must be well under \(1/T\); a common rule is \(\approx 2/(3T)\), so ≈ 667 Hz for \(T=1\) ms.
  • Cost: ~2046 × ~20 cells per PRN, each an N-sample dot product. Done serially in hardware, that's slow; done by FFT, it collapses.

FFT (parallel code-phase) acquisition. Circular correlation over one code period is a convolution, so for each Doppler bin:

\[ \mathbf{r}_{f_D} = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left\{ \mathcal{F}\{x[n]e^{-j2\pi f_D nT_s}\} \cdot \overline{\mathcal{F}\{c[n]\}} \right\} \]

That gives all code phases at once in \(O(N\log N)\). The code FFT is precomputed. A variant FFTs across Doppler instead ("parallel frequency search").

Coherent vs noncoherent integration. SNR grows linearly with coherent time \(T\). But \(T\) is capped three ways:

  • by data-bit flips (20 ms on C/A, unless wiped off or a pilot is used),
  • by Doppler-bin width, since a longer \(T\) needs finer bins and so more cells,
  • by oscillator phase noise and dynamics.

Beyond that you sum \(M\) magnitudes noncoherently, which pays a squaring loss that's worse at low SNR. Detection is a hypothesis test: \(S\) under noise only is \(\chi^2\) with 2M degrees of freedom. You pick a threshold for a target false-alarm rate per cell, then compute the detection probability against a noncentral \(\chi^2\). Sensitivity scales as \(\sim\!10\log_{10}(T_{coh}) + \) (less than) \(5\log_{10}(M)\) dB.

3.2 Tracking: three coupled loops

Each channel runs a set of correlators at offsets around the prompt code phase, usually Early, Prompt and Late, spaced \(\pm d/2\) chip, sometimes very-early/very-late too. They're integrated over \(T\) (1–20 ms, or more on pilots), giving \(I_E, Q_E, I_P, Q_P, I_L, Q_L\).

Bit synchronisation. On C/A, data edges fall on one of 20 possible 1 ms boundaries. Histogram the prompt-sign changes across the 20 positions and pick the peak. With pilots and overlay codes, a secondary-code correlation gives symbol (and often frame) sync directly.

C/N0 estimation. Common choices are the narrowband/wideband power ratio (NWPR, comparing \(|\sum I+jQ|^2\) over M sub-intervals with \(\sum |I+jQ|^2\)) and moment-based estimators. Reported C/N0 drives lock detectors, measurement weighting, and the signal-quality monitors used against spoofing.

Deep dive: vector tracking and why it matters for jammingadvanced

Scalar loops track each satellite independently. Vector tracking (VDLL/VFLL) closes all the loops through the navigation filter instead: the predicted code phase and Doppler for every channel come from the estimated user position, velocity and clock. A weak channel is effectively aided by the strong ones, which buys several dB of tracking threshold. It's also the natural place to fuse an IMU (deep or ultra-tight coupling). The catch: a fault in one channel can contaminate all of them, so integrity monitoring has to live inside the loop.

4Observables & the math

4.1 The three observables

For receiver \(r\), satellite \(s\) and frequency \(i\) (all in metres, \(\lambda_i = c/f_i\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{r,i}^s &= \rho_r^s + c(\delta t_r - \delta t^s) + I_{r,i}^s + T_r^s + b_{r,i} - b_i^s + M_{P} + \varepsilon_P \\ \Phi_{r,i}^s &= \rho_r^s + c(\delta t_r - \delta t^s) - I_{r,i}^s + T_r^s + \lambda_i N_{r,i}^s + \lambda_i(\phi_{r,i}-\phi_i^s) + m_{\Phi} + \varepsilon_\Phi \\ D_{r,i}^s &= -\frac{1}{\lambda_i}\left(\dot\rho_r^s + c(\dot{\delta t}_r - \dot{\delta t}^s) + \dots\right)\ \text{Hz} \end{aligned} \]

Note the sign flip on \(I\). The ionosphere delays the code (group delay) and advances the phase (phase velocity > c) by the same first-order amount. \(N\) is the integer ambiguity, and \(b\) and \(\phi\) are hardware code and phase biases. Typical noise: code ≈ 0.1–1 m, phase ≈ 1–3 mm (approximate, signal-dependent).

4.2 Single-point positioning: Gauss-Newton on four unknowns

Unknown state \(\mathbf{x} = (x, y, z, b)\) with \(b = c\,\delta t_r\). Linearise about a guess \(\mathbf{x}_0\). Let \(\mathbf{e}^s\) be the unit line-of-sight vector from the receiver to satellite \(s\):

\[ \Delta P^s = P^s - \hat P^s(\mathbf{x}_0) \approx -\mathbf{e}^{s\top}\Delta\mathbf{r} + \Delta b, \qquad \mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix} -\mathbf{e}^{1\top} & 1\\ \vdots & \vdots \\ -\mathbf{e}^{n\top} & 1\end{bmatrix}, \] \[ \Delta\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H})^{-1}\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{W}\,\Delta\mathbf{P}, \qquad \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + \Delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}. \]

Iterate to convergence; from Earth's centre that takes about 4–6 iterations. Satellite positions must be evaluated at transmit time \(t_{tx} = t_{rx} - P/c\), then rotated by Earth's rotation during the ~67–86 ms flight time (Sagnac, §4.5). Weights \(\mathbf{W}\) usually follow elevation or C/N0, e.g. \(\sigma^2 = a^2 + b^2/\sin^2 el\).

4.3 Dilution of precision

With equal, uncorrelated range errors \(\sigma_{UERE}\), the state covariance is \(\sigma_{UERE}^2\,\mathbf{Q}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}=(\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{H})^{-1}\). Rotate the position block into local ENU (\(\mathbf{Q}_{ENU} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{Q}_{xyz}\mathbf{R}^\top\)) and then:

\[ \mathrm{GDOP}=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{Q}},\quad \mathrm{PDOP}=\sqrt{q_{EE}+q_{NN}+q_{UU}},\quad \mathrm{HDOP}=\sqrt{q_{EE}+q_{NN}},\quad \mathrm{VDOP}=\sqrt{q_{UU}},\quad \mathrm{TDOP}=\sqrt{q_{bb}}. \]

4.4 Coordinates: ECEF ↔ geodetic

WGS84: \(a = 6378137\) m, \(f = 1/298.257223563\), \(e^2 = f(2-f) \approx 6.69437999\times10^{-3}\), \(N(\varphi) = a/\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\varphi}\).

\[ X = (N+h)\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\quad Y = (N+h)\cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\quad Z = \big(N(1-e^2)+h\big)\sin\varphi. \]

The inverse has no closed form that's both simple and exact. Iterate \(\varphi \leftarrow \operatorname{atan2}\!\big(Z,\ p(1 - e^2 N/(N+h))\big)\) with \(p=\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}\) and \(h = p/\cos\varphi - N\). It converges to sub-mm in 3–4 iterations away from the poles; Bowring's or Heikkinen's closed forms are alternatives. The local ENU rotation is

\[ \mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\lambda & \cos\lambda & 0\\ -\sin\varphi\cos\lambda & -\sin\varphi\sin\lambda & \cos\varphi\\ \cos\varphi\cos\lambda & \cos\varphi\sin\lambda & \sin\varphi\end{bmatrix}. \]

Elevation and azimuth of a satellite come from \(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{r}^s - \mathbf{r}_r)\). This is the same rotation you'd use to place a spatial-audio source or an AR anchor in a local frame. (Precise work also needs to say which realisation, e.g. an ITRF epoch vs a plate-fixed datum, since plates move cm/yr.)

Ionosphere

To first order the ionosphere is dispersive, with group delay \(I_i = \dfrac{40.3\,\mathrm{STEC}}{f_i^2}\) m (STEC in electrons/m², 1 TECU = \(10^{16}\) el/m²).

Worked example: iono delay at L1 \(40.3\times10^{16}/(1575.42\times10^6)^2 = 0.162\) m per TECU at L1 (0.267 m at L2, 0.291 m at L5). A moderate daytime vertical TEC of 30 TECU is ≈ 4.9 m at zenith, and with a slant obliquity factor of ~2–3 at low elevation, ≈ 10–15 m. Solar-maximum afternoons can exceed 100 TECU.

Because \(I \propto 1/f^2\), two frequencies cancel it exactly (to first order). This is the ionosphere-free combination:

\[ P_{IF} = \frac{f_1^2 P_1 - f_2^2 P_2}{f_1^2 - f_2^2} = 2.546\,P_1 - 1.546\,P_2 \quad (\text{L1/L2}). \]

The price is noise amplification of \(\sqrt{2.546^2+1.546^2}\approx 2.98\times\) for equal, independent noise. Higher-order terms (2nd order ~mm–cm, through the geomagnetic field) remain and matter for geodesy.

Troposphere

The troposphere is non-dispersive, so it can't be removed by frequency combinations. The Saastamoinen zenith hydrostatic delay (pressure \(P\) in hPa, latitude \(\varphi\), height \(H\) in km) is:

\[ \mathrm{ZHD} = \frac{0.0022768\,P}{1 - 0.00266\cos 2\varphi - 0.00028\,H}\ \text{m}\quad (\approx 2.3\ \text{m at sea level}), \qquad T^s = m_h(el)\,\mathrm{ZHD} + m_w(el)\,\mathrm{ZWD}. \]

Mapping functions (Niell, GMF, VMF) take you from zenith to slant, roughly \(1/\sin el\) above ~15°. The wet part is variable, so precise processing estimates ZWD as a random walk. That's also how GNSS meteorology retrieves precipitable water.

Relativity

A GPS clock at ~20,200 km altitude runs fast by ≈ 45.7 µs/day (gravitational) and slow by ≈ 7.2 µs/day (velocity), a net of ≈ +38.4 µs/day. Uncorrected, that's ~11 km/day of range error. The fix is in hardware: the onboard 10.23 MHz reference is offset to 10.22999999543 MHz (fractional \(-4.4647\times10^{-10}\)). The remaining periodic part, from orbital eccentricity, is applied by the receiver:

\[ \Delta t_r = F\,e\,\sqrt{A}\,\sin E_k,\qquad F = \frac{-2\sqrt{\mu}}{c^2} = -4.442807633\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{s}/\sqrt{\mathrm{m}} . \]

With \(e = 0.01\) and \(\sqrt{A}\approx 5153.7\ \sqrt{\mathrm m}\): \(|\Delta t_r| \le 22.9\) ns ≈ 6.9 m. Sagnac: in an Earth-fixed frame, the receiver rotates during signal flight. Either rotate the satellite's ECEF position by \(\omega_E\tau\) about Z (\(\omega_E = 7.2921151467\times10^{-5}\) rad/s), or add \(\frac{\omega_E}{c}(x^s y_r - y^s x_r)\). Its theoretical ceiling is ≈ 137 ns (≈ 41 m) for GPS geometry. There's also a small Shapiro path-delay term of ~2 cm (≈), which matters only for precise work.

4.6 Kalman-filter PVT

Snapshot least squares ignores the fact that you were somewhere a moment ago. An (extended) Kalman filter carries state between epochs, e.g. a constant-velocity model with clock bias \(b\) and drift \(d\):

\[ \mathbf{x} = [\,\mathbf{r},\ \mathbf{v},\ b,\ d\,]^\top,\qquad \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{w}_k,\quad \boldsymbol{\Phi} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf I & \Delta t\,\mathbf I & & \\ & \mathbf I & & \\ && 1 & \Delta t\\ &&& 1\end{bmatrix}, \] \[ \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{P}^-\mathbf{H}^\top(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}^-\mathbf{H}^\top + \mathbf{R})^{-1},\quad \mathbf{x}^+ = \mathbf{x}^- + \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{z}-h(\mathbf{x}^-)),\quad \mathbf{P}^+ = (\mathbf I - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{H})\mathbf{P}^- . \]

Measurements \(\mathbf z\) are pseudoranges (rows of \(\mathbf H\) as above) and Doppler-derived range rates, whose rows are \([\,\mathbf{0},\ -\mathbf{e}^\top,\ 0,\ 1\,]\). The clock's process noise comes from its Allan-variance coefficients (\(h_0, h_{-2}\)), which is why a better oscillator directly improves coasting through outages. The same structure extends naturally to RTK (float ambiguities as states), PPP (tropo and ambiguities), and loosely or tightly coupled INS.

Deep dive: minimal snapshot solvercode
import numpy as np
def spp(sat_pos, pr, x0=np.zeros(4), iters=8):
    """sat_pos: (n,3) ECEF at transmit time (already Sagnac-rotated); pr: (n,) corrected pseudoranges [m]"""
    x = x0.astype(float)
    for _ in range(iters):
        d   = sat_pos - x[:3]
        rho = np.linalg.norm(d, axis=1)
        H   = np.hstack([-d / rho[:, None], np.ones((len(pr), 1))])
        dz  = pr - (rho + x[3])
        dx, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(H, dz, rcond=None)
        x  += dx
        if np.linalg.norm(dx[:3]) < 1e-4: break
    Q = np.linalg.inv(H.T @ H)
    return x, np.sqrt(np.trace(Q))   # state, GDOP

5RTK & precise positioning

To get centimetres you have to use carrier phase, which means solving for \(N\). Differencing against a nearby reference receiver removes most of the nuisance terms, so \(N\) becomes estimable as an integer in seconds instead of minutes.

5.1 Single and double differences

\[ \Delta(\cdot)_{rb}^s = (\cdot)_r^s - (\cdot)_b^s, \qquad \nabla\Delta(\cdot)_{rb}^{sk} = \Delta(\cdot)_{rb}^s - \Delta(\cdot)_{rb}^k \] \[ \nabla\Delta\Phi_{rb}^{sk} = \nabla\Delta\rho_{rb}^{sk} + \lambda\,\nabla\Delta N_{rb}^{sk} - \nabla\Delta I + \nabla\Delta T + \nabla\Delta m + \varepsilon . \]

The DD ambiguity \(\nabla\Delta N\) is a true integer, provided both satellites transmit on the same wavelength. That's the GLONASS FDMA complication: DDs between different channels leave a non-integer residual from \((\lambda_s - \lambda_k)N\) and the inter-frequency biases. For short baselines, \(\nabla\Delta I\) and \(\nabla\Delta T \to 0\), and the model is purely geometric plus integers.

Differencing correlates the measurements, since every DD shares the reference satellite, so the DD covariance is \(\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{D}^\top\), not diagonal. Getting this right matters for the ambiguity search.

5.2 Integer ambiguity resolution

  1. Float solution. Estimate the baseline \(\mathbf b\) and the ambiguities \(\mathbf a\) as reals by weighted least squares or an EKF over code and phase DDs. This gives \(\hat{\mathbf a}\), with a covariance \(\mathbf Q_{\hat a}\) that's highly correlated and elongated.
  2. Integer estimation (ILS). \[ \check{\mathbf a} = \arg\min_{\mathbf z\in\mathbb Z^n} (\hat{\mathbf a}-\mathbf z)^\top \mathbf Q_{\hat a}^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf a}-\mathbf z). \] The search ellipsoid is very elongated, which makes a naive search explode. LAMBDA applies an integer, unimodular (\(|\det \mathbf Z| = 1\)) decorrelating transform \(\hat{\mathbf z}=\mathbf Z^\top\hat{\mathbf a}\), \(\mathbf Q_{\hat z}=\mathbf Z^\top\mathbf Q_{\hat a}\mathbf Z\), built from integer Gauss transformations and permutations on the \(\mathbf{LDL}^\top\) factors. That makes the ellipsoid nearly spherical, so a depth-first search over the conditional variances \(d_i\) is fast. Then map back: \(\check{\mathbf a} = \mathbf Z^{-\top}\check{\mathbf z}\).
  3. Validation. Accept the fix only if the best candidate is clearly better than the second. A common test is the ratio test \(\dfrac{\|\hat{\mathbf a}-\check{\mathbf a}_2\|^2_{\mathbf Q}}{\|\hat{\mathbf a}-\check{\mathbf a}_1\|^2_{\mathbf Q}} \ge c\), with \(c\) typically ~2–3 (approximate convention; RTKLIB's default is 3). Modern practice prefers fixed-failure-rate thresholds. Also check the success rate from the ADOP \(= |\mathbf Q_{\hat a}|^{1/(2n)}\).
  4. Fixed solution. Condition the baseline on the integers: \[ \check{\mathbf b} = \hat{\mathbf b} - \mathbf Q_{\hat b\hat a}\mathbf Q_{\hat a}^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf a} - \check{\mathbf a}). \] Precision jumps from dm to mm–cm. A wrong fix is worse than no fix: it's a confident cm-precise answer that's off by whole wavelengths. That's why the validation and partial ambiguity resolution (fixing only a reliable subset) matter.

Wide-lane / narrow-lane cascade

\[ \lambda_{WL} = \frac{c}{f_1-f_2},\qquad \lambda_{NL} = \frac{c}{f_1+f_2} . \]

For L1/L2, \(\lambda_{WL} = 86.2\) cm and \(\lambda_{NL} = 10.7\) cm; for L1/L5, \(\lambda_{WL} = 75.1\) cm. Fix the long-wavelength WL first, because it's easy against dm-level noise. Then the narrow-lane / L1 ambiguity follows from \(N_1 - N_2 = N_{WL}\). With three frequencies (GPS L1/L2/L5, Galileo E1/E5a/E5b/E6), an extra-wide-lane of several metres (L2–L5: ≈ 5.86 m) can be fixed nearly instantly. That's the basis of TCAR/CIR cascades.

5.3 Cycle slips

A cycle slip is a jump of an integer number of cycles in \(N\) when the PLL momentarily loses lock (from shadowing, a vibration spike or low C/N0). The integer from that point on is new, so it must be detected and either repaired or re-estimated.

  • Geometry-free (GF) combination, \(L_{GF} = \Phi_1 - \Phi_2 = (\lambda_1 N_1 - \lambda_2 N_2) - (I_1 - I_2) + \dots\): geometry and clocks are gone and the ionosphere varies smoothly, so a jump of more than a few cm between epochs flags a slip. Its blind spot is slip pairs with \(\lambda_1\Delta N_1 \approx \lambda_2\Delta N_2\) (e.g. 9/7 on L1/L2).
  • Melbourne-Wübbena (MW): \[ L_{MW} = \frac{f_1\Phi_1 - f_2\Phi_2}{f_1-f_2} - \frac{f_1P_1 + f_2P_2}{f_1+f_2} = \lambda_{WL}N_{WL} + \text{(biases)} + \varepsilon. \] This is geometry-free, ionosphere-free and clock-free, so it's a noisy but unbiased estimate of the WL ambiguity. A running mean that jumps by more than a few σ flags a WL slip. GF and MW are complementary, which is why most detectors run both.
  • Single frequency: compare time-differenced phase with Doppler-integrated range or an IMU-predicted delta range. Receivers also export a loss-of-lock indicator (LLI) and lock-time counters (they go out in RTCM, §6).

5.4 Baseline length: why RTK has a range

As the baseline grows, DD residuals of the ionosphere (and, less so, of the troposphere and orbits) grow too. A rough rule is ~1–2 ppm of residual ionospheric error, i.e. mm per km (≈; much worse in storms, near the equatorial anomaly, or at high latitude). Once that residual is a significant fraction of a narrow-lane cycle, integer fixing fails. Typical practical ranges (≈, conditions-dependent) are ~10 km for single-frequency and a few tens of km for multi-frequency fast fixes. Beyond that you have to estimate the ionosphere (iono-weighted or iono-float models), accept longer fix times, or use a network. The often-quoted accuracy spec "≈ 1 cm + 1 ppm horizontal" carries this baseline dependence in its second term.

6Correction communication protocols

6.1 RTCM 10403.x (RTCM 3) framing

RTCM SC-104 version 3 is the lingua franca for differential corrections. The transport frame is tiny and deliberately simple:

// CRC-24Q, as used by RTCM3 (polynomial 0x1864CFB, init 0, no reflection, no final XOR)
uint32_t crc24q(const uint8_t *p, size_t n){
  uint32_t crc = 0;
  while (n--) { crc ^= (uint32_t)(*p++) << 16;
    for (int i = 0; i < 8; i++) { crc <<= 1; if (crc & 0x1000000) crc ^= 0x1864CFB; } }
  return crc & 0xFFFFFF;
}

Observables are split into a per-satellite "rough range" (integer and fractional ms) and per-signal fine offsets, so redundant high-order bits are sent once per satellite.

Four constellations at 1 Hz therefore need ≈ 5.5 kbit/s (MSM4) or ≈ 8.8 kbit/s (MSM7), plus a 1005/1033 every few seconds. Real numbers vary with satellite and signal counts (computed estimate, ±).

6.3 NTRIP: RTCM over HTTP

NTRIP (Networked Transport of RTCM via Internet Protocol) is a thin HTTP-like streaming protocol. A caster relays streams from servers (base stations) to clients (rovers). Streams are named by mountpoint, and the conventional port is 2101.

# NTRIP v1 (HTTP/1.0-ish, ICY reply)
GET /MOUNT HTTP/1.0
User-Agent: NTRIP ExampleClient/1.0
Authorization: Basic dXNlcjpwYXNz

ICY 200 OK
<raw RTCM bytes …>
# client may write NMEA $GPGGA upstream
# on the same socket (needed for VRS)
# NTRIP v2 (proper HTTP/1.1)
GET /MOUNT HTTP/1.1
Host: caster.example:2101
Ntrip-Version: Ntrip/2.0
User-Agent: NTRIP ExampleClient/2.0
Authorization: Basic dXNlcjpwYXNz
Ntrip-GGA: $GPGGA,…

HTTP/1.1 200 OK
Content-Type: gnss/data
Transfer-Encoding: chunked

GET / returns the sourcetable: SOURCETABLE 200 OK in v1, Content-Type: gnss/sourcetable in v2. It's a semicolon-separated list of CAS, NET and STR records. STR rows describe each mountpoint: format (e.g. RTCM 3.3), message list with rates, constellations, lat/lon, whether NMEA uplink is required, authentication, and bitrate. Version 2 adds chunked transfer, which makes it proxy- and firewall-friendly. It also defines optional RTSP/RTP and plain UDP transports for lower overhead.

There's no security in the base protocol beyond HTTP Basic auth, which is plaintext unless wrapped in TLS. TLS has become common, and corrections are an underrated spoofing and integrity surface: a rover that trusts unauthenticated corrections can be steered by whoever controls them.

6.4 Latency and age of corrections

The rover has to pair base and rover observations from the same epoch. There are two strategies:

  • Synchronous (matched-epoch): buffer the rover's data until the base's arrives. This is the most accurate, but adds the full link latency.
  • Low-latency (extrapolated): predict the base observations forward to now. This adds error that grows with the age of corrections.

Over seconds, differential errors change slowly. Satellite clock wander dominates, and orbits and the atmosphere evolve over minutes. So cm-level RTK tolerates a few seconds of age, and degrades progressively over tens of seconds (≈, receiver- and conditions-dependent). Rovers enforce a maximum age, after which they drop to float or DGNSS. The link's job is low jitter and low loss more than raw speed.

6.5 Links at the protocol level

  • Licensed UHF/VHF data radio: narrow channels (12.5/25 kHz), GMSK/4-FSK-class modulations, a few kbit/s up to ~19.2 kbit/s (≈). It's one-way broadcast, so there are no ACKs. Robustness comes from forward error correction and interleaving, and from repeating static messages (1005/1033) periodically so a rover that missed them can recover. RTCM payloads are usually wrapped in the radio's own framing with its own FEC.
  • LoRa (CSS) on ISM bands: chirp spread spectrum trades rate for link margin. SF7 at 125 kHz gives ≈ 5.5 kbit/s raw, and SF12 gives ≈ 0.3 kbit/s. The PHY payload maximum is 255 bytes. The computed time-on-air for one 172-byte MSM4 frame at SF7/125 kHz/CR 4/5 is ≈ 277 ms, and the MSM7 frame from §6.2 (275 bytes) doesn't fit in one packet at all. So multi-constellation 1 Hz RTK over LoRa means aggressive trimming: MSM4 only, fewer signals, static messages at a low rate, and splitting by constellation. Regional duty-cycle rules (e.g. 1% in parts of EU868) and dwell-time limits (US915) are often the real constraint. Use the LoRa PHY directly (point-to-multipoint broadcast), not LoRaWAN's uplink-oriented MAC.
  • Satellite L-band and in-signal broadcast carry compact SSR (below), since a one-way, wide-area channel is exactly SSR's sweet spot.

6.6 Compact SSR formats

SSR fits bandwidth-starved channels because each error component gets its own update rate and resolution:

  • Orbits and code/phase biases: every ~30–60 s.
  • Clocks: every ~5 s.
  • Atmosphere: as gridded or polynomial coefficients, every ~30 s.

Examples of compact encodings, all given as approximate rates:

  • Galileo HAS on E6-B. HAS data takes about 448 bit/s per satellite, carried in Reed-Solomon-encoded pages so a receiver can reassemble a message from any sufficient subset of satellites (erasure coding across space).
  • QZSS CLAS on L6 (2 kbit/s).
  • BeiDou PPP-B2b.
  • RTCM SSR, the open IGS-SSR, and SPARTN, used for IP or L-band delivery.

The design moves of compact SSR are worth stealing for any low-rate telemetry link: send deltas against a broadcast reference (IOD-tagged), use variable resolution, mask unused fields with bitmaps, and put integrity and validity flags on every block.

7Multipath

7.1 Mechanism

Reflections off the ground, buildings, a car roof or water arrive later than the direct signal by an excess path \(\delta\), with relative amplitude \(\alpha\) and phase \(\theta\). The correlator sees the sum of shifted copies:

\[ R_c(\tau) = R(\tau) + \sum_m \alpha_m\, R(\tau - \delta_m)\, e^{j\theta_m}. \]

The DLL balances early and late on this distorted peak, so it settles at the wrong place. Meanwhile the PLL tracks the phase of the phasor sum. The effects differ:

  • Code: error up to a large fraction of the correlator spacing, i.e. tens of metres for C/A with a wide correlator (Figure 7).
  • Carrier: \(\delta\phi = \arctan\dfrac{\alpha\sin\theta}{1+\alpha\cos\theta}\), bounded by \(\arcsin\alpha\). Even at \(\alpha \to 1\) it never exceeds \(\lambda/4\) (≈ 4.8 cm at L1), and typically it's mm to a cm.
  • Time signature: as geometry changes, \(\theta\) rotates, so static multipath shows up as a sinusoid with a period of minutes, which averages down only slowly. GPS ground tracks repeat about every sidereal day (≈ 4 min earlier each solar day), so static-site multipath repeats too. That makes sidereal filtering possible.

7.2 Mitigation in the receiver

  • Narrow correlator (d ≈ 0.05–0.2 chip): the envelope scales roughly with d. Requires front-end bandwidth well beyond the 2 MHz main lobe.
  • Double-delta / "strobe" / high-resolution correlators: combine two early-late pairs, e.g. \(D = (E_1 - L_1) - \tfrac{1}{2}(E_2 - L_2)\), to synthesise a sharper effective reference waveform. This zeroes the envelope for delays beyond a small fraction of a chip. The cost is more noise, because you throw away signal energy.
  • MEDLL (Multipath Estimating DLL): fits direct and reflected components (\(\alpha_m, \delta_m, \theta_m\)) to many correlator outputs by maximum likelihood, then tracks the direct path only. It's the parametric version of the idea above.
  • Signal design: BOC and wideband 10.23 Mcps signals (L5, E5) have intrinsically smaller envelopes. E5 AltBOC is excellent because of its very wide bandwidth.
  • Carrier smoothing (Hatch filter): \(\bar P_k = \tfrac{1}{M}P_k + \tfrac{M-1}{M}\big(\bar P_{k-1} + \Phi_k - \Phi_{k-1}\big)\). This averages code multipath down using the clean carrier. On single frequency it suffers from iono divergence (code and phase iono have opposite signs), so the time constant has to be limited (≈ 100 s in aviation use).

7.3 Mitigation in the antenna: polarisation and pattern physics

  • RHCP. GNSS signals are right-hand circularly polarised. A single specular reflection off a good conductor at steep incidence reverses the handedness to LHCP, which a well-made RHCP antenna rejects (by its axial ratio, i.e. cross-pol discrimination, ≈ 10–20+ dB near zenith; it degrades toward the horizon). Near grazing incidence, or off dielectrics like ground and water past the Brewster angle, the reflection is elliptical and only partly rejected.
  • Choke ring. Concentric grooves about λ/4 deep present a high-impedance surface at the operating frequency. That suppresses surface waves and edge diffraction, giving a sharp gain roll-off at and below the horizon, where ground multipath arrives. It's bandwidth-limited (multi-band designs compromise), and big: ~λ-scale at L-band. Ground planes and resistive or absorbing ground planes are smaller cousins.
  • Phase-centre stability. At mm level the antenna's own phase-centre offset and variation vs elevation and azimuth (PCO/PCV) are a systematic term. They're calibrated and published, which is why RTCM 1033 carries the antenna descriptor.

7.4 Detection

  • C/N0 / SNR patterns: interference between direct and reflected paths makes SNR oscillate with \(\sin(el)\). For a horizontal reflector at height \(h\), the oscillation frequency is \(2h/\lambda\) cycles per unit \(\sin(el)\). Comparing SNR against a clean-sky elevation template flags multipath per satellite. Run the other way, this is GNSS interferometric reflectometry (GNSS-IR), used to measure snow depth, soil moisture and tide height.
  • Code-minus-carrier: \(P - \Phi = 2I + M_P + \text{const}\). After removing the slowly varying ionosphere and the constant, what's left is code multipath. The MP1/MP2 combinations use dual-frequency data to remove the ionosphere exactly.
  • Residual-based: large or periodic post-fit residuals, plus elevation and C/N0 weighting or exclusion in the filter.

8Interference & jamming

8.1 What processing gain buys and what it doesn't

Despreading turns a narrowband jammer into wideband noise, spreading it by the code. Its impact on effective C/N0 is the classic Kaplan result:

\[ \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\!eff} = \left[\left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)^{-1} + \frac{J/S}{Q\,R_c}\right]^{-1}, \]

where \(R_c\) is the chip rate. \(Q\) is a spectral-shape factor: \(Q\approx1\) for narrowband/CW on a spectral line, ≈ 1.5 for a matched BPSK-like spread jammer, and ≈ 2 for wideband Gaussian noise.

Worked example: a 10 mW jammer at 1 km EIRP +10 dBm, isotropic. Free-space loss at 1575.42 MHz and 1 km is \(32.44 + 20\log_{10}(1575.42) + 20\log_{10}(1) \approx 96.4\) dB, so J ≈ −86.4 dBm. Against S = −128.5 dBm, J/S ≈ 42 dB. With C/N0 = 43.5 dB-Hz, \(Q = 2\) and \(R_c = 1.023\) Mcps, the effective C/N0 ≈ 21 dB-Hz, below the tracking threshold of a typical non-aided receiver. Free-space numbers are optimistic for the victim, because terrain and buildings usually help. Signal choice matters too: in this formula the 10× higher chip rate of L5 or E5a buys ≈ 10 dB more jamming margin against wideband jammers than C/A does.

8.3 Signal-processing defences

  • Pulse blanking: zero the samples whose amplitude exceeds a threshold set above the Gaussian noise envelope. Loss ≈ \(-10\log_{10}(1 - \text{duty})\) dB on the wanted signal plus a little noise reshaping. It needs a multi-bit ADC and a fast AGC that isn't captured by the pulses.
  • Notch filtering: adaptive IIR notches (lattice or LMS) that track a CW tone, or frequency-domain adaptive filtering (FFT the block, excise bins far above the median, IFFT). Chirps need time-frequency excision, since a fixed notch is useless against a sweeping tone.
  • AGC monitoring: because the GNSS signal is ~20 dB below noise, the AGC gain is set by noise plus interference alone. A gain drop of a few dB is therefore an early, constellation-independent interference detector. It's also the cheapest anti-spoofing power check (§9). Pair it with C/N0 drops across all satellites at once.
  • More bits, more linearity: a 1-bit ADC has almost no dynamic range for interference. Multi-bit ADCs and high-IP3 front-ends are what make excision possible at all.

8.4 Spatial defences: CRPA and null steering

A controlled reception pattern antenna (CRPA) with \(N\) elements forms \(y = \mathbf w^H\mathbf x\). The power-inversion approach minimises output power subject to a constraint on one element, \(\min_{\mathbf w}\mathbf w^H\mathbf R_{xx}\mathbf w\) s.t. \(w_1 = 1\). Since GNSS signals are below noise, this nulls only strong interferers and needs no knowledge of their direction. If satellite directions are known, MVDR or beam-steering puts gain on each satellite:

\[ \mathbf w_{MVDR} = \frac{\mathbf R_{xx}^{-1}\mathbf a(\theta_s)}{\mathbf a^H(\theta_s)\mathbf R_{xx}^{-1}\mathbf a(\theta_s)} . \]

An N-element array can place up to \(N-1\) independent nulls, often tens of dB deep (≈, depends on calibration, bandwidth and platform). Space-time adaptive processing (STAP) adds tap delays per element to handle wideband and multipath-spread jammers. Arrays also distort the carrier phase and group delay they deliver, which has to be calibrated out for precise positioning.

9Spoofing & anti-spoofing

Jamming denies service, and it's loud. Spoofing replaces service, and if it's done well the receiver reports a confident, wrong answer. The open civil signals are fully specified (that's the point of an ICD), so anyone with an SDR can synthesise them.

9.1 Attack taxonomy

9.2 Detection without cryptography

  • Power and AGC checks. To capture tracking loops, a spoofer usually has to be a few dB above the authentic signal. Watch for: total in-band power up (AGC gain down) while C/N0 rises; all satellites with suspiciously similar C/N0; C/N0 not following the elevation-dependent antenna pattern; and absolute C/N0 above anything physically plausible from orbit.
  • Signal-quality monitoring (SQM) and distortion. During carry-off the authentic and counterfeit peaks overlap and the correlation function distorts. Monitor shape metrics across multiple correlators. Two standard ones are the delta metric \(\Delta = \dfrac{I_E - I_L}{2I_P}\) and the ratio metric \(\dfrac{I_E + I_L}{2I_P}\). Track their statistics, and watch for vestigial authentic peaks after takeover. Real multipath produces similar distortions, so thresholds are environment-dependent.
  • Angle of arrival. With two or more antennas, the single-difference carrier phase between antennas for satellite \(s\) should equal \(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf e^s/\lambda + N + \text{bias}\), with \(\mathbf b\) the antenna baseline vector. For a single-source spoofer, every satellite shows the same difference. A cheap variant: move a single antenna through a known motion and check that the carrier-phase changes differ per satellite as they should.
  • Consistency with other sensors and models.
    • An IMU or odometry bounds how fast the position can legitimately change.
    • A good local oscillator in holdover bounds clock jumps and drift. This is especially relevant for timing receivers, where a 1 µs drag is a classic attack goal.
    • Doppler should match ephemeris-predicted range rates.
    • Ephemeris and almanac should be checked against cached or out-of-band copies.
    • Cross-check across constellations and frequencies: spoofing L1 alone while L5 and E5 remain authentic creates inconsistencies.
  • RAIM-style residual tests (§10) catch crude or partial spoofing, where a subset of satellites is false. A self-consistent full-constellation spoof has small residuals by construction, so RAIM alone is not anti-spoofing.

9.3 Cryptographic approaches

  • Navigation message authentication (NMA), e.g. Galileo OSNMA (§2.7). It authenticates the data, so a simulator can't invent ephemeris. It doesn't authenticate the ranging: meaconing and replay with a small delay remain possible within the time-uncertainty bound. The defence pairs it with tight time and sensor checks.
  • Spreading-code authentication (SCA) / Chimera. Chimera, proposed by AFRL for GPS L1C, punctures the pilot spreading code with cryptographically generated marker chips. Their key derives from a digitally signed navigation message and is disclosed after broadcast (bit commitment and time binding). The receiver stores raw samples, waits for the key, regenerates the markers and correlates. That authenticates the signal itself, not just the data. Per the literature, a "slow channel" key arrives in-band on the order of minutes, and a "fast channel" uses out-of-band delivery on the order of seconds. The remaining threat is SCER-style chip estimation, which marker design makes costly.
  • Encrypted codes (GPS M-code, Galileo PRS), at the conceptual level: the entire spreading sequence is generated from a secret key, so without the key you can neither generate the signal nor, at useful SNR, estimate it chip by chip. Access control is key management and tamper-resistant receivers. It resists everything except meaconing and very-high-gain SCER, at the cost of keyed hardware and the lack of open access.
Deep dive: a defence-in-depth stack for an embedded receiverdesign
  1. Monitor AGC and per-satellite C/N0 vs elevation templates. It's cheap and catches most crude attacks.
  2. Cross-check across multiple constellations and frequencies, and check consistency with an IMU or oscillator holdover.
  3. Use OSNMA where available. Enforce its time-synchronisation requirement with an independent time source (network time with authentication, or a disciplined oscillator).
  4. Run SQM on a few extra correlators per channel.
  5. If the platform allows two antennas, add an AoA check.
  6. Authenticate the correction stream (§6.3) too. An open NTRIP feed is a steering input.

Psiaki & Humphreys (2016) give an attack-defence matrix showing which of these defeats which attack. It's the best one-page way to see the gaps.

10Integrity & time

Accuracy is how close you usually are. Integrity is the guarantee that, when you're wrong by more than a stated bound (the protection level), you'll be told within a time-to-alert, with a specified tiny probability of missed detection. Aviation made this rigorous.

10.1 RAIM

With \(n > 4\) measurements the least-squares residual \(\mathbf r = (\mathbf I - \mathbf H\mathbf G)\mathbf y\), where \(\mathbf G = (\mathbf H^\top\mathbf W\mathbf H)^{-1}\mathbf H^\top\mathbf W\), carries redundancy. In the fault-free case,

\[ \mathrm{SSE} = \mathbf r^\top\mathbf W\mathbf r \sim \chi^2_{n-4} . \]

Set a threshold \(T\) for the allowed false-alarm probability. A single-satellite bias \(b_i\) maps into horizontal error through that satellite's slope:

\[ \mathrm{slope}_i = \frac{\sqrt{G_{1i}^2 + G_{2i}^2}\;\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{ii}}},\qquad \mathbf S = \mathbf I - \mathbf H\mathbf G . \]

The horizontal protection level is \(\mathrm{HPL} = \max_i \mathrm{slope}_i \cdot \sqrt{\lambda_{\det}}\), where \(\lambda_{\det}\) is the non-centrality needed to meet the missed-detection probability. Detection needs ≥ 5 satellites and exclusion (FDE) ≥ 6. RAIM assumes a single fault at a time, which multi-constellation operation and constellation-wide faults break.

10.2 ARAIM

Advanced RAIM uses solution separation. It computes the all-in-view solution \(\hat{\mathbf x}_0\) and subset solutions \(\hat{\mathbf x}_k\), each excluding one satellite or a whole constellation per a fault-hypothesis list, then tests \(|\hat x_{0} - \hat x_{k}| \le T_k\) per axis. The protection level is built from fault-tolerant bounds weighted by prior fault probabilities (\(P_{sat}\), \(P_{const}\)) and nominal error models (URA/URE, bias terms). Those parameters come from an Integrity Support Message (ISM); GPS civil ISM formats were added to IS-GPS-200/705/800 by the June 2026 change notices on the GPS.gov ICD page.

10.3 SBAS principles

A ground network of reference stations estimates satellite orbit and clock errors, plus the ionosphere on a grid. GEO satellites broadcast them on L1 at 250 bps (rate-½ convolutional coding, 500 sps, ~1 s messages of 250 bits with CRC-24Q), at a C/A-like chip rate, and the GEO itself is also a ranging source. Key message types:

  • Fast corrections (MT 2–5) for clock-like errors.
  • Long-term corrections (MT 25) for orbit and slow clock errors.
  • Ionospheric grid delays with GIVE bounds (MT 18/26), on a 5° grid of ionospheric grid points (IGPs).
  • Integrity and degradation parameters (MT 6, 7, 10).

The user forms per-satellite variances from UDRE, GIVE, tropo and receiver terms, and computes protection levels \(\mathrm{VPL} = K_V\sigma_V\), \(\mathrm{HPL} = K_H\sigma_{major}\). For precision approach, \(K_V = 5.33\) and \(K_H = 6.0\), per the SBAS MOPS. Time-to-alert is seconds. Dual-frequency multi-constellation (DFMC) SBAS moves to L5/E5a and removes the iono grid, because users cancel the ionosphere themselves.

10.4 GNSS as a timing source

  • Time scales: GPS Time is continuous, with no leap seconds; it was aligned to UTC in 1980 and is now 18 s ahead. Galileo GST is aligned with GPST at its epoch; BeiDou BDT started in 2006 and runs 14 s behind GPST; GLONASS time tracks UTC(SU) + 3 h including leap seconds. The broadcast UTC parameters (\(A_0, A_1, \Delta t_{LS}\), and the leap-second schedule) let the receiver output UTC, and each system broadcasts the inter-system offset (e.g. GGTO).
  • Timing receivers: at a surveyed, fixed position, the only unknown is \(b\), so one satellite is enough in principle (more for robustness and RAIM). The output is a 1PPS edge plus a time-of-day message, and a sawtooth correction for the PPS quantisation. A disciplined OCXO or rubidium oscillator is steered with a long time constant, which also gives holdover through outages and spoofing alarms. Typical PPS accuracy relative to UTC is tens of ns (≈, depends on antenna-cable delay calibration and receiver).
  • Time transfer: common-view uses two sites observing the same satellite at the same time and exchanging their measurements, so the satellite clock cancels. All-in-view with precise products and carrier-phase time transfer go below 1 ns. This is how national labs compare clocks for UTC (BIPM Circular T).
  • Why timing matters for security: cellular base stations, power-grid synchrophasors and financial timestamping often discipline from GNSS. A slow time carry-off is the most consequential civil spoofing scenario, and holdover-based consistency checks (§9.2) are the first line of defence.
