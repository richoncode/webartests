Titanium Colour Physics

Anodizing volts and laser power both grow the same nanometre-thin TiO₂ film on titanium — they just take completely different physical routes to get there. This page compares them side by side, then lets you drive both with a shared simulator.

Two Machines, One Optical Trick

Neither process adds pigment. Both grow a transparent titanium dioxide (TiO₂) layer a few tens to a few hundred nanometres thick on top of the bare metal. Light reflects off both the top of that film and the metal underneath it; the two reflections interfere, and whichever wavelengths cancel out determine what colour survives. Thickness is the only dial that matters — anodizing and lasering are just two different ways of turning that dial.

This is the same optical mechanism covered for stainless steel in SS304 Colour Physics (Cr₂O₃ instead of TiO₂) and in Sequence 12: Thin-Film Interference — titanium follows the same interference math, with a slightly different refractive index and a much wider, more saturated colour range.

Two Ways to Grow the Film

Electrochemical anodizing and laser marking reach the same colour space from opposite directions — one drives oxide growth with an electric field, the other with heat.

🔋

Electrochemical Anodizing

  • Driver: DC voltage across the part, immersed in an electrolyte bath (commonly a mild phosphate or borate solution).
  • Mechanism: The applied field drives oxide growth directly — thickness tracks voltage in a near-linear "anodizing constant," roughly ~2.5 nm per volt for titanium.
  • Coverage: Whole part at once, uniform to the geometry of the electrode.
  • Speed: Seconds per part once the bath and rig are set up.
  • Reference: see the real voltage/colour chart at EDC Specialties.
📡

IR Laser Marking

  • Driver: Localized heat from an IR fibre laser — no bath, no electrolyte, no field.
  • Mechanism: Heat drives thermal oxidation. Growth follows a parabolic/Arrhenius law like SS304's: thickness scales roughly with the square root of the heat dose, not linearly.
  • Heat dose knobs: the same real settings as every other Learning Lasers lesson — Power, Speed (mm/s), Line Density (LPCM). Oxide growth is fundamentally power × time — but hands-on testing found Power is the dial that actually controls colour; Speed and LPCM are held at a fixed reference rather than swept.
  • Coverage: Pixel-by-pixel, programmable — gradients, text, and per-pixel colour control that a bath can't do.
  • Job time: Seconds to tens of seconds depending on area and the fixed density/speed reference.

Calibrating the Laser Side to the Real IR Module

The same three-stage model as SS304's parameters section — energy in, peak temperature, then Arrhenius oxide growth — run with titanium's own numbers instead of steel's.

// xTool F2 IR module (real spec, same hardware as every other Learning Lasers page): P_max = 5 W @ 1064 nm // Titanium absorbs 1064nm markedly better than SS304 (50-60% vs. 38%): α_Ti = 0.55 // Titanium's real density & specific heat (vs. SS304's 8000 kg/m³, 500 J/kg·K): ρ_Ti = 4506 kg/m³ · c_p = 523 J/kg·K // Hands-on investigation: repeated passes / higher line density did NOT deepen the oxide the // way a naive cumulative-dose model predicts. Power was the reliable, repeatable control. // So Speed and LPCM are held at the tested reference point, and Power is the only free variable: LPCM = 1000, speed = 200mm/s (fixed) → t_exp = (1000/10) / 200 = 0.5 s/mm² (fixed) // Energy injected, same focal-skin model (40µm depth, η = 0.65): Q = P × α_Ti × t_exp ΔT = Q·η / (m·c_p), m = 1mm² × 40µm × ρ_Ti ≈ 1.80×10⁻⁷ kg // Example: 8% of 5W = 0.4W, at the fixed 1000 LPCM / 200mm/s reference: t_exp = 0.5s → Q = 0.11 J → ΔT ≈ 758°C → T_peak ≈ 778°C → k_p·t_exp → ~127nm → Pale Green // TiO2 (rutile) parabolic growth — activation energy from published cyclic-oxidation studies // (155-205 kJ/mol across sources; 170 used here): k_p(T) = A · exp(−E_a / RT), E_a ≈ 170 kJ/mol

Because titanium is lighter (roughly half SS304's density, so less thermal mass to heat) and absorbs more 1064nm light, a given power setting pushes it far hotter than steel at the same exposure time — which is exactly why real titanium annealing markers run at a fraction of the power used for SS304 structural colour, and why power, not speed or density, is the dial that actually needs fine control.

A real chip card, at this exact reference point

A physical titanium colour chip card — made at this exact 1000 LPCM / 200mm/s reference, each chip labelled with its power% — beats any tuned model, so the simulator's swatch now reads directly off it (interpolated between chips) instead of the theoretical Arrhenius colour. Two things it shows that the model alone wouldn't predict:

  • The clean gold→bronze→purple→blue→teal→sage progression matches thin-film interference reasonably well from ~13% to ~41% power.
  • Above ~41%, the card desaturates into greys and mauves rather than continuing into more saturated "2nd-order" colours the way the pure interference model implies — consistent with the oxide surface roughening or scattering at higher heat rather than staying a clean, smooth interference film. The simulator flags this range as "Desaturating" instead of showing a modelled thickness that wouldn't mean much.
What's real here and what isn't: the 5W ceiling, absorptivity, density, specific heat, and the activation energy range are cited, published values. The fixed 1000 LPCM / 200mm/s reference point, "power is the dominant control" finding, and the colours themselves (13-61% range) come from hands-on testing and a photographed reference card — real measurement, not a model. The Arrhenius pre-exponential factor (A) is still tuned, used only for the Peak Temp readout and the modelled-thickness estimate in the vivid range — it no longer drives what colour is actually shown. The chip colours themselves are a careful visual read of a photograph, not a colorimeter measurement, and above 61% is genuinely untested.

Three Things That Surprise People

🟢

Yes — a real Deep Green

Published anodizing charts put a rich, saturated Deep Green around ~90–110V — noticeably richer than the paler green earlier in the sequence. That's the ~250nm stop above; earlier "Learning Lasers" work on SS304 never reaches a colour this saturated at comparable thickness, because titanium's oxide keeps producing strong interference further into the 2nd order than chromium oxide does.

🚫

Red doesn't exist here

Neither anodizing nor laser marking can produce a true, saturated red on titanium. Thin-film interference at TiO₂'s refractive index just doesn't have a thickness where red reinforces without other wavelengths reinforcing alongside it — the closest you get is pink/magenta (a red-blue mix), never a clean red.

Finish changes everything: the same voltage or laser setting looks completely different depending on surface prep — a mirror-polished surface gives vivid, saturated jewel tones, while a bead-blasted or brushed surface scatters light and produces the same hue as a muted pastel/satin. Neither the volts nor the nanometres change; only how cleanly the reflected rays stay coherent does.
