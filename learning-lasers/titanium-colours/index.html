Titanium Colour Physics

Anodizing volts and laser power both grow the same nanometre-thin TiO₂ film on titanium — they just take completely different physical routes to get there. This page compares them side by side, then lets you drive both with a shared simulator.

Two Machines, One Optical Trick

Neither process adds pigment. Both grow a transparent titanium dioxide (TiO₂) layer a few tens to a few hundred nanometres thick on top of the bare metal. Light reflects off both the top of that film and the metal underneath it; the two reflections interfere, and whichever wavelengths cancel out determine what colour survives. Thickness is the only dial that matters — anodizing and lasering are just two different ways of turning that dial.

This is the same optical mechanism covered for stainless steel in SS304 Colour Physics (Cr₂O₃ instead of TiO₂) and in Sequence 12: Thin-Film Interference — titanium follows the same interference math, with a slightly different refractive index and a much wider, more saturated colour range.

Two Ways to Grow the Film

Electrochemical anodizing and laser marking reach the same colour space from opposite directions — one drives oxide growth with an electric field, the other with heat.

🔋

Electrochemical Anodizing

  • Driver: DC voltage across the part, immersed in an electrolyte bath (commonly a mild phosphate or borate solution).
  • Mechanism: The applied field drives oxide growth directly — thickness tracks voltage in a near-linear "anodizing constant," roughly ~2.5 nm per volt for titanium.
  • Coverage: Whole part at once, uniform to the geometry of the electrode.
  • Speed: Seconds per part once the bath and rig are set up.
  • Reference: see the real voltage/colour chart at EDC Specialties.
📡

IR Laser Marking

  • Driver: Localized heat from an IR fibre laser — no bath, no electrolyte, no field.
  • Mechanism: Heat drives thermal oxidation. Growth follows a parabolic/Arrhenius law like SS304's: thickness scales roughly with the square root of the heat dose, not linearly.
  • Heat dose knobs: laser power (peak temperature) and overscan — how many times the galvo re-traces the same line, standing in for "time in the bath."
  • Coverage: Pixel-by-pixel, programmable — gradients, text, and per-pixel colour control that a bath can't do.
  • Speed: Seconds to tens of seconds per pass set, depending on area and overscan count.
