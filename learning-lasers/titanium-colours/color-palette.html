The Titanium Colour Palette

One physical dial — the thickness of a transparent oxide film — produces every colour titanium can show, in a fixed order, whether you get there with a laser or a bath of electrolyte. This page walks that order stop by stop, then spends its last section on the one colour that resists showing up cleanly: green.

Recap

Why One Number Makes a Whole Palette

Full derivation lives on Titanium Colour Physics — the short version, so this page stands on its own:

  • Light reflects off two surfaces here — the top of the transparent oxide film, and the metal underneath it.
  • Those two reflections interfere. Depending on the oxide's optical thickness, some wavelengths cancel out and others reinforce.
  • Whatever wavelengths survive that cancellation is the colour you see — no pigment, no dye, just geometry.
  • Thickness is the only input that matters. Anodizing volts and laser power are just two different ways of setting it — see the dual-mode simulator for both paths side by side.

The Sequence

Walking the Palette, Stop by Stop

Same idealised model as the simulator's colour stops — smooth, uncorroded oxide, growing from nothing. Scroll sideways; the story is the trend, not any single swatch.

Two orders, one wraparound: the palette doesn't run out at magenta — it's already showing the start of a second interference order by ~180nm (2nd-order Gold), then Orange, then Pink/Magenta again before a real Deep Green appears around ~250nm. Same physics, just enough extra thickness to fit another full cycle of cancellation into the same visible-light window.

The Exception

Why Green Is Elusive

Every other colour on the strip above shows up bold when the film is smooth and coherent. Green is different — and both real cards independently show the same failure, from opposite directions.

Where Green Sits

(longer wavelength) Yellow / orange side →
~400nm~700nm

This is the same rainbow a prism splits sunlight into — titanium's interference colours just walk along it as oxide thickness increases. Green sits almost exactly in the middle, with blue and cyan on its short-wavelength side (thinner oxide — Blue and Teal, earlier in the palette strip above) and yellow and orange on its long-wavelength side (thicker oxide — Yellow-Green and 2nd-order Gold, later in the strip). That's exactly what "blue side" and "yellow side" mean in the two approaches below: the two directions you can dial oxide thickness from to try to land on green — a little more than Teal, or a little less than Yellow-Green.

The physics — still just a phase shift, nothing more exotic. The mechanism is exactly what it sounds like: two reflections — one off the top of the oxide, one off the oxide/metal boundary underneath — travel different path lengths. That path difference creates a wavelength-dependent phase difference, phase(λ) = 4πnd/λ. Some wavelengths arrive back in phase and reinforce; others arrive out of phase and cancel. No separate filter, no extra component — just interference from that one phase shift, the same as any other thin film.

"Highpass," "lowpass," and "bandpass" below describe the shape of that interference plotted across the narrow 400–700nm window we can actually see — not a different mechanism. Because phase(λ) depends on 1/λ, thicker oxide sweeps more of one full interference cycle across that window:

  • Thin oxide: only a small arc of the underlying cosine fits inside 400–700nm. A small arc near the middle of a cosine looks almost like a straight ramp — reflectance just climbs or falls across the visible range with no turning point inside it. That monotonic ramp is the "highpass/lowpass" shape: one edge of the spectrum suppressed, the other left alone, and forgiving of small thickness errors because there's nothing sharp to miss.
  • Thicker oxide: enough of that same cosine fits inside the window that a complete peak or trough now lands inside 400–700nm instead of outside it. The curve rises, turns over, and falls again — a genuine turning point with both flanks visible. That's the "bandpass" shape green needs, and it only appears once thickness sweeps enough phase to fit a full turning point in view — a narrower target, and one where small errors move the turning point noticeably.

Same phase-shift interference the whole way through. What changes with thickness is only how much of one cosine cycle happens to land inside the slice of spectrum we can perceive.

// Two-beam phase across the visible window: x(λ) = 4π·n·d / λ (n = TiO₂ refractive index, d = physical thickness) // Total phase swept from 400nm to 700nm: Δx = 4π·n·d · (1/400 − 1/700) = 4π·n·d · 0.00107 nm⁻¹ // Δx = π → half a cycle fits → ONE turning point at the edge → highpass/lowpass (easy: gold, blue, purple) n·d = π / (4π · 0.00107) ≈ 233 nm → d ≈ 233 / 2.5 ≈ ~93 nm (using TiO₂'s n ≈ 2.5) // Δx = 2π → a FULL cycle fits → a turning point appears INSIDE the window → bandpass (hard: green) n·d = 2π / (4π · 0.00107) ≈ 467 nm → d ≈ 467 / 2.5 ≈ ~187 nm // That ~187nm threshold lands right where this palette places Yellow-Green/2nd-order Gold (160-180nm) — // consistent with green needing the thickest, narrowest-tolerance condition of any colour on the strip.

There's a second, deeper reason, and it has nothing to do with roughness: even a perfectly smooth film's green notch would look less saturated than blue's or red's notch of the same physical depth. The eye's colour receptors each integrate light over a fairly wide band of wavelengths — roughly 100nm or more. A highpass/lowpass edge (blue, red, gold) still leaves one whole side of the spectrum strong, so it comfortably out-votes whatever leaks through from the suppressed side. A bandpass notch (green) is narrow enough that the receptor bands on either side of it always catch some of the light the notch didn't suppress, diluting it. Same modulation depth, less perceived saturation, before any real-world imperfection gets involved at all.

How your eyes perceive colour. Your eye doesn't sample one wavelength at a time — each of its three cone types integrates light over a broad band (roughly ±50nm or more). Turning the toggle below on convolves every curve on this page with a representative ~50nm averaging window before reading off the resulting colour, from one shared setting.

Checked directly (peak-to-trough amplitude before vs. after blur): the order 2 example below (a broad ~160nm-wide hump) only loses about a quarter of its amplitude — barely visible. The order 6 example in the "tighter bands" box further down loses over 80%, because a genuinely narrow feature has far less width for a 50nm window to average over. That's the real, verifiable place to watch this toggle work — not "green specifically," but narrow specifically, which is a related but more precise claim: green's first attempt (order 2) happens to still be broad, so it survives blur about as well as blue or red do at this thickness. It's the higher orders — and, in the real chip-card data above, the roughened real oxide — where this effect actually bites.

All four diagrams above — the three examples and the slider — are driven by the exact same formula and the exact same colour-derivation function (a simplified wavelength-weighted sum, not the illustrative named palette used elsewhere on this page). Drag the slider to 100nm, 124nm, or 152nm and its curve will exactly match the corresponding example above — same numbers, same code path, by construction.

What is Order? The orange "Ord." badge on every diagram answers "which order am I looking at?" — counted directly from the curve, not looked up from a table: Order 1 is a plain monotonic edge with no turning point inside 400–700nm (a highpass/lowpass shape). Order 2 has exactly one interior peak or dip (a bandpass or notch — green's category). Order 3+ means the film is thick enough that more than one full turning point fits inside the visible window at once — genuinely more complex behaviour than a single hump. This is a property of the curve itself, so it stays fixed even when the perception toggle blurs how that curve is seen.
Why do the bands get tighter at higher orders? Because phase depends on 1/λ, the wavelength width of one full interference cycle shrinks as thickness grows: differentiating phase(λ) = 4πnd/λ gives a cycle width of roughly Δλ ≈ λ² / (2·nd) — inversely proportional to thickness. Order 2 fits exactly one cycle-width's worth of turning point into the 300nm-wide visible window, so that feature can be relatively broad. Order 6 has to fit several complete cycles into that same 300nm span, so each individual peak and dip has to be proportionally narrower to make room. Higher order doesn't just mean "harder to hit" (the maths box above) — it means the target itself keeps shrinking the further out you go, which is also why this page's Reference Charts and the anodizing "2nd-Order Gold, Orange, Pink/Magenta" sequence read as progressively less saturated than the first-order run through Gold-Purple-Blue. Toggle human perception above and watch this specific row — order 2's broad hump barely moves, but order 6's already-narrow peaks nearly flatten, because a fixed-width blur only erases detail finer than itself.

Does It Get Smoother Again Past Green, or Is Red Just More Tolerant?

Worth being precise here, because it's actually both, and they're doing different jobs:

  • Roughness itself doesn't reverse. On this card, once accumulated heat crosses the threshold that roughens the surface, it doesn't get smooth again further out — 70–91% (dark maroon-brown) is no smoother than 60–66% (drab olive). It's a one-way threshold tied to heat dose, not something thickness alone undoes.
  • But red, blue, and gold really are more tolerant of that same roughness. Roughness scatters light, which adds a wavelength-flat "grey" component on top of whichever colour the thickness alone would predict — diluting saturation for every colour, not just green. The difference is margin: a highpass/lowpass colour (blue, gold, red) has a big, high-contrast signal to dilute, so a given amount of scattering barely dents its saturation. Green's bandpass signal is smaller and narrower to begin with — per the perception argument above, it's already running close to the "looks grey" line even on a hypothetically smooth film. The same scattering that blue shrugs off is enough to push green over that line.
A real, hands-on data point on the roughness side of this: crosshatch fill — two passes at different angles over the same area, exactly what produced this card — has been observed to give noticeably better saturation than a single unidirectional raster pass at the same power and line density. The likely mechanism: a single-direction raster leaves a periodic ridge-and-groove texture running along the scan direction, which scatters light strongly and directionally. A second pass at a different angle re-hits those ridges from the side, knocking down some of that periodic texture into something finer and more isotropic — less like a diffraction grating, more like a uniformly frosted surface. That's a real saturation win, and unlike everything in the "Finishing Possibilities" section below, it costs nothing extra — it's a scan-strategy choice, not a post-process step, so there's no risk of removing oxide or shifting the hue. It's also clearly not sufficient on its own — this exact card is already crosshatched, and it still never reaches a real green. Whatever roughening survives cross-hatching is happening at a finer scale than scan-line geometry can fix — plausibly genuine oxide microstructure (grain growth, phase changes) from the heat itself, which is where the ideas below come in.
Why anodizing gets there and laser marking usually doesn't: a real, saturated Deep Green exists on published anodizing charts around ~90–110V (~250nm) — see the palette strip above. A bath keeps building a smooth, uniform oxide all the way out to that thickness. Laser-grown oxide reaches comparable thickness through heat instead, and by the time it's thick enough, accumulated thermal roughening has usually already started. Two different growth mechanisms, reaching the same theoretical thickness, arriving in very different surface conditions.
How to spot roughness with nothing but a point light. A smooth surface reflects a point source (phone LED, laser pointer) as one sharp dot. A rough surface scatters it into a soft, blurry glow — that scattered light is the same "grey wash" that dilutes saturation, made visible.
  • Reflect the point source off the surface at a moderate angle, against a dark background.
  • Tilt the piece slowly. Sharp dot that tracks cleanly = smooth. Blurry glow that smears or wanders = rough.
  • Always compare against a known-vivid reference area or control coupon — "blurrier than the reference" is the real signal.

Same diagnostic as the "point-light reflection" row on Protecting Titanium's Smoothing vs. Thickening table — run it before and after any technique below.

An Open Experiment

Improving Saturation (Theory)

Avoiding Roughness (Before Colour Is Reached)

The techniques below all react to roughness after the fact. There's a theoretical case that some of it is avoidable in the first place, by how line spacing (LPCM) relates to the physical beam width — the same relationship LPCM & DPI: The Density Factor covers generally, applied specifically to scattering rather than thermal dose.

// Line spacing from LPCM: spacing (mm) = 10 / LPCM. "Critical" spacing = beam width itself. LPCM_critical = 10 / beam_width_mm // Using the real xTool F2 IR spot size already cited on Titanium Colour Physics' gap analysis (~0.03mm / 30µm): LPCM_critical = 10 / 0.03 ≈ 333 lines/cm // Both real chip cards on this page are already well past that threshold, not near it: Card 1 — 1000 LPCM → spacing = 10/1000 = 0.010mm (10µm) — beam is ~3× the spacing: heavy overscan Card 2 — 834 LPCM → spacing = 10/834 = 0.012mm (12µm) — beam is ~2.5× the spacing: heavy overscan

Three regimes, each its own theory of scattering — labelled deliberately as theories, not conclusions, because every one is cheap to actually go test on scrap:

Theory: "Frost scatter"
Spacing wider than the beam — low LPCM

A focused spot's intensity tapers off gradually, not sharply. Wider spacing means the gaps between lines are only ever reached by that low-intensity tail — never a full dose, never zero — leaving a graded smear of half-formed oxide instead of one clean thickness.

PredictsA soft, frosted haze rather than a hue — not just a paler version of the intended colour.
TestRun the same recipe at a deliberately low LPCM and check specifically for haze, not just paleness.
Theory: Critical Fill
Spacing equal to the beam width

Lines just touch, edge to edge, with minimal double-exposure. Two adjacent Gaussian profiles still don't sum to a perfectly flat total, so a periodic seam remains — but the finest, most regular version this geometry can produce.

PredictsThe mildest texture achievable without deliberately overlapping passes — a baseline, not a cure.
TestUse as the reference point every other LPCM value below should be compared against.
Theory: Overscan Cuts Both Ways
Spacing narrower than the beam

Every point gets hit by more than one pass, blending adjacent lines into something more continuous — genuinely reducing the macro periodic signature. The risk: more overlap usually means more total heat, and heat is what roughens the microstructure. But dose and peak temperature aren't the same lever: overscanning at correspondingly lower power could trade a few hot, sharp-edged passes for many gentler, overlapping ones, at similar or lower total dose — replacing each line's abrupt hot/cold boundary with a shallow, gradual ramp. Neither real card here tuned power down to compensate for its high LPCM, so this trade-off is untested, not ruled out.

PredictsCould go either way — smoother from blending, or rougher from added heat, depending on whether power was reduced to compensate.
TestHold total dose roughly constant while trading LPCM up and power down, then check the point-light test above for sharper or blurrier.

None of this has been tested against real saturation measurements — it's a theoretical reading of the same LPCM-vs-spot-size relationship the density page already establishes, aimed at scattering instead of thermal dose.

Smoothing the Surface (After Colour Is Reached)

If roughness-driven scattering is what's costing green (and, to a lesser extent, every other colour) its saturation, the natural next question is whether you can fix that after the fact — by polishing — without undoing the thing you're trying to preserve. This section is explicitly untested speculation, reasoning from the same physics above and the theories on Protecting Titanium's Interference Colours, not a verified result.

What actually causes scattering — it isn't only roughness. Smoothing is one physical mechanism: the surface geometry itself gets flatter — sharp peaks rounded, tiny ridges reduced, valleys partly filled, debris removed. Less scattering is the optical result: light reflecting in a more organised way instead of being sprayed in many directions. Smoothing is one way to get there, but it isn't the only one — several of the ideas in this section, cross-hatching especially, plausibly work through a completely different mechanism, without the surface geometry becoming any flatter at all.

Ways scattering could drop without true smoothing:

  • More uniform oxide thickness. The physical roughness could be identical, but if the oxide's thickness becomes more even from point to point, the interference colour it produces becomes more consistent too — richer, more coherent saturation, with the same peaks and valleys still there.
  • Less directional texture. A single raster pass leaves grooves running one direction — a strong, streaky scattering pattern. A second pass at a different angle can make that texture more isotropic without making it any shallower. Less directionally organised scattering, not necessarily less scattering in total.
  • Residue cleanup. A second pass can burn off, reflow, or redistribute loose haze and redeposited material sitting on top of the real oxide — reducing scattering from that residue layer alone, with the underlying oxide geometry untouched.
  • Index/oxide filling. A second pass might grow a little extra oxide into the existing valleys, or coat rough features more evenly — the physical peaks can still be there, but the optical contrast between peak and valley drops, which is what actually determines how much a given bump scatters.
  • Microtexture becomes better tuned. A surface can occasionally end up microscopically rougher by some measures and still scatter less in the specific viewing/lighting condition that matters, simply because the texture became more regular rather than shallower.

Practically: the point-light test above tells you whether scattering dropped. It can't tell you which of these five mechanisms did it — that needs the real microscopy and spectrometer measurements in Protecting Titanium's experimental protocol.

Could Polishing Boost Green (and Red) Saturation?

Plausibly, yes. If saturation loss is mostly scattered light diluting the real interference signal, then anything that restores specular reflection — without changing oxide thickness — should restore saturation, the same way a thin oil temporarily restored brilliance on IPA-dulled titanium (Theory B on the Protecting Titanium page: index-matching / smoothing, not a hue change). Green should show the largest relative gain of any colour, precisely because it's operating closest to the "looks grey" threshold — a small improvement in coherence is the difference between "recognisably green" and "olive," where the same improvement on an already-vivid blue is barely visible.

The Problem: Everything That Touches the Surface Is a Risk

This is the exact fragility already mapped out on the Protecting Titanium page, just approached on purpose instead of by accident:

  • Remove oxide material, and thickness drops — which changes hue, not just saturation. Over-polish and you don't get a vivid green, you get a vivid something-else. This is Theory A run deliberately: pull optical thickness down, and the colour moves.
  • Abrade or collapse the oxide's own texture, and you get more scattering, not less — the opposite of the goal. This is Theory C: mechanical contact on a fragile as-grown film tends to make it worse before it makes it better.
  • Leave any residue behind, and it joins the optical stack — a permanent film has the same hue-shifting risk as the wax observation this whole colour family keeps referencing (Theory F).

A successful polish: saturation recovers, hue doesn't move

The failure mode: polish too much, and thickness — and hue — moves too

Electropolishing is a real chemical and electrical hazard, not just an optical experiment. Titanium electropolishing electrolytes are typically concentrated acid mixtures — commonly sulfuric/hydrofluoric acid combinations — energised with meaningful DC current. Hydrofluoric acid specifically is a contact poison whose symptoms can be delayed well past the point of exposure, which is exactly the kind of hazard that "very light" and "very low current" doesn't make go away. Follow your specific electrolyte's SDS and the equipment manufacturer's instructions exactly, use the PPE they specify, and don't attempt this without them in hand. Background reading, not a substitute for either: Electropolishing (process overview) and Hydrofluoric acid (why this specific hazard is unusually dangerous). Anodizing's electrolytes and voltages are comparatively milder, but still a live acid bath under DC power — see Anodizing for the process and its own handling considerations.

Measure saturation and hue before and after every single one; a technique that helps saturation but also moves hue has failed the actual goal, even if it "looks better" at a glance.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Optical thicknessPhysical thickness × refractive index — the one number that determines hue in thin-film interference.
Interference orderHow many full cancellation cycles fit within the visible spectrum at a given thickness. Titanium's palette repeats with a second, less saturated cycle after ~160nm.
Reflectance notchThe wavelength band interference cancels out. A wide notch near one end of the spectrum gives a bold, forgiving colour; a narrow notch in the middle (needed for green) is fragile.
Desaturating regimeWhere a real oxide's surface roughens from heat, scattering light instead of reflecting it coherently — collapses saturation regardless of what hue the thickness alone would predict.
Optical homogenizingMaking a surface's optical response — thickness, refractive index, or roughness — uniform enough across its area that reflected light from every point interferes coherently instead of scattering as incoherent "noise." This is the actual physical goal behind "smoothing the surface": an oxide can be exactly the right average thickness for green and still fail to look green if it isn't optically homogeneous at the scale light interacts with it.
Texture homogenizingThe narrower, physical cousin of optical homogenizing: making the surface's actual topography — the peaks, valleys, and grooves left by however it was grown or scanned — more uniform and less directionally periodic, without necessarily changing average thickness at all. Cross-hatching and maximising LPCM (in the table below) are both texture-homogenizing moves. Neither guarantees optical homogenizing on its own — a finer, more isotropic texture still has to be shallow enough, relative to the wavelength of light, to stop scattering coherently rather than reflecting it.
Low-fluenceFluence is energy delivered per unit area (J/mm² — the same energy-density idea covered on Power & Speed). "Low-fluence" means deliberately staying well below the energy density that grows or changes the oxide — enough to gently heat and mobilise just the top of the surface for smoothing, not enough to meaningfully add thickness or restart oxide growth. The margin between "smooths" and "starts changing the colour again" is exactly the part that would need real testing.
