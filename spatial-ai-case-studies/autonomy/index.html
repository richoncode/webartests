Open-Source Autonomy Stacks

A working reference on ArduPilot/PX4-class autonomy — the software, simulators, hardware, and unresolved edges — anchored to a real target application: a small, custom-coded drone surveying pedestrian areas for COLMAP-style 3D reconstruction.

First: what "DJI Neo 2-class" actually means

The scorecard and hardware goal below lean on terms like "sub-250g class," "A1 subcategory," and "omnidirectional obstacle sensing." Here's what those mean and what the Neo 2 itself actually is, before comparing anything to it.

Weight-class regulation (why 250g is the line everyone designs to)

JurisdictionSub-250g classWhat it buys you
US — FAA Part 107"Category 1" operations over peopleLegal to fly over uninvolved people if the aircraft (with everything attached) is ≤0.55 lb / 250g and has no exposed rotating parts that could lacerate skin. Recreational flyers under 250g skip registration entirely; commercial Part 107 flyers must register regardless of weight.
EU — EASA Open CategoryClass C0, subcategory A1C0 covers toy-grade aircraft under 250g (max ~19 m/s); A1 is the lowest-risk subcategory, allowed over uninvolved individuals (never over crowds). No Remote ID needed for C0 in the Open category — but a camera onboard still requires operator registration regardless of weight.

Neither rule set cares about MAVLink, GCS, or open vs. closed firmware — they're purely about mass and prop exposure. That's why "sub-250g" keeps coming up as a hardware target independent of the software stack: it's the line between "needs a waiver/case-by-case approval to fly over people" and "doesn't."

What the Neo 2 actually is

TraitNeo 2
Weight151g (160g with the optional digital transceiver/O4 module) — inside the sub-250g bracket above
Prop guardsFully enclosed integrated guards clip over each propeller — not an add-on accessory, part of the stock airframe

Caveat: the Neo 2 launched globally in Nov 2025 but is not sold in the US market. For a US-based build, FAA Category 1 (250g, no lacerating rotating parts) is the rule that actually applies — the EASA A1/C0 framing is useful for understanding "why 250g," not as the regulatory target itself.

Why "open-source" instead of consumer

Consumer drones (DJI Mini/Neo, Tello) are closed end-to-end: no MAVLink, no flashable firmware, no SDK hooks for a palm-class airframe. Getting genuine custom-software control — a scriptable flight path, a triggered camera loop, a live obstacle-avoidance decision — means building on an open architecture instead: an open autopilot, a standard telemetry protocol, and (optionally) a companion computer running your own code.

That architecture is vehicle-agnostic by design — the same stack flies a quadcopter, a fixed-wing, or a ground rover — which is the core reason it's worth learning once rather than per-product.

The three layers, at a glance

🧠

Autopilot firmware

ArduPilot (ArduCopter / ArduPlane / ArduRover) or PX4. Runs on a Pixhawk-class flight controller, handles stabilization, EKF state estimation, and failsafes.

🔗

MAVLink

The lightweight binary protocol every layer speaks — telemetry streaming plus request/acknowledge microservices for commands like arm, takeoff, or trigger-camera.

💻

GCS & companion computer

QGroundControl / Mission Planner for humans; a Raspberry Pi or Jetson running your own Python/ROS 2 code for autonomous, camera-driven behavior.

MAVLink in one paragraph

MAVLink is a header-only, binary-packed protocol built for weak radio links. Vehicles continuously broadcast telemetry (position, attitude, battery) as a publish-stream, and separate request/acknowledge microservices handle anything needing confirmation — uploading a mission, arming, or triggering a camera. Every device gets a System ID and Component ID, so one radio link can carry multiple vehicles and multiple payloads (autopilot, gimbal, camera) without collision.

Practical upshot: your companion-computer script doesn't talk to the drone directly — it talks MAVLink, the same language QGroundControl speaks, over a serial UART or a routed network link.

Don't bridge power pins between the two — feed each from its own regulated source.

Configuration steps:

  1. On the Pi: raspi-config → disable serial console shell, keep hardware UART on.
  2. In QGroundControl: set SERIAL2_PROTOCOL=2 (MAVLink) and matching baud (e.g. 921600).
  3. On the Pi: run mavlink-router or pymavlink/DroneKit to forward and script against the link.

Test before you fly: SITL

Software-In-The-Loop runs the exact autopilot binary on your laptop instead of the physical board. A physics engine feeds it simulated IMU/GPS data; your GCS connects over MAVLink exactly as it would to real hardware. Zero hardware cost, and it's how you validate mission logic, custom scripts, and failsafe behavior first.

macOS quick start

1. Install QGroundControl — official .dmg, drag to Applications.

2. Install Gazebo via Homebrew:

brew tap osrf/simulation
brew trust osrf/simulation
brew install gz-harmonic   # or gz-jetty for latest

3. Run server and GUI in separate terminals (macOS needs them split):

gz sim -v4 -r shapes.sdf     # terminal 1: physics server
gz sim -g                    # terminal 2: GUI client

4. Bridge in ArduPilot SITL and confirm QGC picks up the telemetry:

sim_vehicle.py -v ArduCopter -f gazebo-iris --model JSON --map --console
# in the console: mode guided → arm throttle → takeoff 5

Default SITL "home" is Canberra, Australia (HOME_LAT/HOME_LON); override with -L <name> from ArduPilot's Tools/autotest/locations.txt, or custom coordinates, so QGC renders the real survey site.

Automated capture loop (Python / MAVLink)

Distance-based triggering (not a timer) keeps photo spacing consistent regardless of wind or speed variation:

from pymavlink import mavutil

master = mavutil.mavlink_connection('/dev/ttyAMA0', baud=921600)
master.wait_heartbeat()

# Fire the shutter every 1.5 m of ground track, computed onboard
master.mav.command_long_send(
    master.target_system, master.target_component,
    mavutil.mavlink.MAV_CMD_DO_SET_CAM_TRIG_DIST,
    0, 1.5, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0
)

Why the obvious cheap sensor fails

The Himax HM01B0 (320×320 monochrome, <4mW, the sensor behind Crazyflie's AI-deck) is a tempting way to keep the airframe tiny — but it fails COLMAP's Structure-from-Motion pipeline on three counts:

Resolution

320×320 over an open field means a huge ground-sample-distance — grass and dirt become textureless blocks with no distinct SIFT features.

Monochrome

Losing color strips the chromatic contrast (white lines vs. green turf vs. brown dirt) that generates extra feature matches.

Rolling shutter

Low-altitude jitter + rolling shutter + longer low-light exposure = motion blur, which COLMAP simply rejects.

Minimum viable payload instead: a color, ~1080p/4K global- or good rolling-shutter camera (Pi Camera Module 3, stripped action cam, or OAK-D color sensor).

QGroundControl's Plan → Pattern → Survey computes this grid automatically from camera specs + altitude + overlap %, and inserts the MAV_CMD_DO_SET_CAM_TRIG_DIST commands for you.

Camera angle: why straight-down (or straight-forward) isn't enough

A camera pointed dead-down over a flat field — nadir, meaning aimed straight at the point directly beneath the aircraft, perpendicular to the ground — or dead-forward along the flight line, both create weak geometry for SfM: parallel/near-parallel rays make it hard for COLMAP to separate camera altitude from focal length, and a forward-looking lens has almost no parallax near the center of frame (classic "motion along the optical axis" degeneracy). The confirmed target — see — is a camera angled 30–50° left or right of the flight line: as the vehicle flies its waypoint path, each 3D point on the ground gets seen from several different angles in sequence, instead of one repeated nadir or forward view.

Camera orientation relative to flight lineParallaxCOLMAP result
Nadir (straight down, 90°) — QGroundControl's default survey gridLow (near-parallel rays into the ground plane)Weak — altitude/focal-length ambiguity, "doming" distortion; each point seen from basically one angle
Dead-level, straight forwardVery low (center degenerate)Poor — frequent depth drift
30–50° forward, pitched downHighGood — solid for narrow corridors/paths
Sideways (left or right of flight line), 30–50° downVery high (clean lateral)Best — matches the target spec; strong, consistent parallax the whole pass

Nadir is the default because it's what QGroundControl's Plan → Pattern → Survey grid produces out of the box — the 30–50° spec means that default grid isn't what gets flown here; the mission needs to be built (or the camera mounted) to hold the angled view instead.

Three ways to get a 30–50° angled camera on a waypoint pass:

  1. Gimbal + MAVLink mount control — MAV_CMD_DO_MOUNT_CONTROL, yaw set to the chosen side (left or right) and pitch 30–50° down, relative to the vehicle nose.
  2. Vehicle yaw override — fly the multirotor crabbed/sideways down the track via MAV_CMD_CONDITION_YAW / WP_YAW_BEHAVIOR, front camera fixed at the chosen offset.
  3. Fixed angled mount (no gimbal) — simplest for a lightweight micro build; camera bolted 30–50° off the frame's forward axis to the chosen side, pitched down to match.

None of these three have actually been flown at the 30–50° spec yet — see .

The requirement: avoid obstacles, keep making progress

Per the , simply stopping in place when something's detected doesn't satisfy the mission — a waypoint survey that halts and waits stops collecting the angled imagery it's out there for. The target behavior is reroute around the obstacle and continue toward the next waypoint, not just brake. That rules out "stop-in-place" as an acceptable avoidance mode on its own and points at ArduPilot/PX4's path-replanning behaviors (BendyRuler, Dijkstra) below.

Data flow into the flight stack

  1. Sensor plugs into the companion computer (USB/CSI/UART).
  2. Companion computer builds a local occupancy grid (e.g. ROS 2 nav2 / octomap).
  3. Results ride back to the flight controller as MAVLink DISTANCE_SENSOR or OBSTACLE_DISTANCE messages.
  4. ArduPilot/PX4 reacts. Stop-in-place is the simplest mode, but it stalls mission progress — the goal calls for BendyRuler or Dijkstra (OA_TYPE=1/2, AVOID_ENABLE, AVOID_MARGIN), which plan a detour around the obstacle and keep heading toward the next waypoint.

Always validate at low speed in an open field before running any low-altitude pass near pedestrians.

Key areas needing further development

Everything above is a components-and-architecture reference synthesized from research — none of it has been built or flight-tested end to end yet. These are the specific gaps to close next.

Full-spec weights are a component sum, not a weighed build

Adding up spec-sheet weights for battery, camera, GPS, sensors, and (where not confirmed stock) a cage puts both the ArduCopter micro-whoop (~145–155g) and the Pavo Pico II/INAV build (~125–130g) — see — under or at the edge of the Neo 2's 151–160g on paper. But several line items (whether the "72g" ArduWhoop figure is bare or already includes a battery; the exact IO-board, GPS-puck, and telemetry-radio weights on the cinewhoop fallback; the estimated bolt-on cage weight itself) came from conflicting, incomplete, or extrapolated sources. Nobody has actually assembled either candidate with its camera and sensors and put it on a scale.

INAV's MAVLink support is telemetry-only — confirm before relying on it

The Pavo Pico II / INAV path plans missions via MSP (mwp or INAV Configurator), not MAVLink waypoint upload — QGroundControl can monitor but not plan a mission on it. If QGroundControl-native planning is a hard requirement, ArduCopter/PX4 remain the only fit, which pushes back toward the heavier candidates.

Pavo Pico II's INAV target support isn't confirmed

Its FC is STM32F405-based, a chip family INAV broadly supports, but no source found here confirms an INAV unified-target build actually exists for this specific board. Needs a direct check against INAV's target list before treating it as a real option rather than a plausible one.

Angled-camera rig is theory only

The parallax argument for a 30–50° left-or-right camera is sound photogrammetry theory, but no gimbal config, fixed-mount bracket, or actual flight test has confirmed it in practice for this airframe size — including whether the same mount can be switched between left and right without a rebuild.

Overlap math hasn't been run against a real site

The front-lap/side-lap formulas are generic. They need to be run against the actual camera's FOV, the 6' altitude, and real site dimensions (e.g. a specific baseball field) to produce a concrete waypoint count, flight time, and battery budget.

Shutter-trigger wiring is unspecified

MAV_CMD_DO_SET_CAM_TRIG_DIST assumes a MAVLink-addressable shutter. The physical trigger path for a Pi Camera Module 3 (GPIO signal vs. software-timed capture synced to telemetry) hasn't been chosen or tested.

Reroute-and-continue avoidance is unverified

No sensor has actually been selected, mounted, or bench-calibrated. AVOID_MARGIN/PRX1_TYPE values here are defaults, not measured stopping-distance guarantees near people — and nobody has confirmed BendyRuler/Dijkstra actually replans and resumes progress toward the next waypoint on this hardware, rather than just slowing or stopping.

Regulatory review is missing entirely

Flying at ~6' over pedestrian areas raises FAA Part 107 (operations over people), local ordinance, and liability questions that this reference doesn't touch — required before any real-world flight.

DJI Neo/Tello status should be periodically re-checked

Confirmed closed as of this writing (no MAVLink, no flashable firmware, Tello's own UDP SDK is the only code path). Community jailbreak/SDK status can shift — worth a re-check before ruling consumer hardware out again.

No end-to-end build exists yet

Frame, flight controller, companion computer, camera, and avoidance sensor have never been assembled, wired, and parameter-tuned together. This page is a build plan, not a build report.

Flight stack & control

ArduPilotOpen-source autopilot firmware suite. Vehicle-specific builds: ArduCopter (multirotors), ArduPlane (fixed-wing/VTOL), ArduRover (ground vehicles).
PX4Another open-source autopilot firmware, backed by the Dronecode Foundation, built around the uORB messaging bus.
MAVLinkLightweight binary telemetry/command protocol used between autopilots, ground stations, and companion computers.
GCSGround Control Station — software like QGroundControl or Mission Planner used to plan missions and monitor a vehicle in flight.
EKFExtended Kalman Filter — the estimator that fuses noisy IMU/GPS/compass data into one best-guess position, velocity, and attitude.
Companion computerA separate onboard computer (e.g. Raspberry Pi) running custom code, talking to the flight controller over MAVLink — distinct from the flight controller itself.
Waypoint missionA pre-planned sequence of coordinates (and actions) the autopilot flies autonomously, without manual piloting input.
ROS 2Robot Operating System 2 — middleware commonly run on a companion computer for navigation, vision, and sensor fusion.
INAVA lighter-weight open-source flight-control firmware common on small FPV/whoop-class boards; plans missions via its own MSP protocol rather than MAVLink.
MSPMultiWii Serial Protocol — INAV's native communication protocol, used by the INAV Configurator and mwp for configuration and mission planning.
mwpAn open-source ground-station app built specifically for INAV, supporting map-based waypoint mission planning over MSP.

Simulation

SITLSoftware In The Loop — running the actual autopilot firmware on a regular computer against a simulated vehicle, instead of real hardware.
GazeboThe most widely used open-source 3D robotics simulator; bridges to ArduPilot/PX4 SITL for physics and sensor simulation.

Hardware & airframe

Cinewhoop / whoopA small ducted-propeller FPV quadcopter frame class, sized for close-proximity flying.
AUWAll-Up Weight — the total ready-to-fly weight of a drone including battery, camera, and everything attached.
ELRSExpressLRS — an open-source, low-latency radio-control link protocol common on FPV receivers.
FPVFirst-Person View — flying via a live video feed from an onboard camera, as opposed to line-of-sight.

Survey & photogrammetry

COLMAPAn open-source Structure-from-Motion (SfM) and multi-view stereo pipeline that reconstructs 3D models from overlapping photos.
SfMStructure from Motion — reconstructing 3D geometry and camera positions from a set of 2D photos of the same scene.
GSDGround Sample Distance — the real-world size one pixel represents on the ground; smaller GSD means more detail.
Front-lap / side-lapThe percentage overlap between consecutive photos along a flight line (front-lap) or between adjacent flight lines (side-lap).
NadirCamera aimed straight down, perpendicular to the ground, at the point directly beneath the aircraft.
ParallaxThe apparent shift in a point's position between two photos taken from different viewpoints — the signal SfM uses to compute depth.
Rolling shutterA camera readout method that scans a frame line-by-line rather than all at once, which can smear a moving scene.

Obstacle avoidance

ToFTime of Flight — a sensor that measures distance by timing a light pulse's round trip to a surface and back.
LiDARA sensor that measures distance using laser pulses, often scanning across an area to build a distance map.
VPSVision Positioning System — camera-based position hold that estimates movement from a downward or forward camera feed, without relying on GPS.
BendyRuler / DijkstraArduPilot's two path-replanning algorithms for obstacle avoidance — both steer around an obstacle and continue toward the next waypoint, instead of just stopping.

Regulatory

FAA Part 107 Cat. 1US rule permitting flight over people if the aircraft weighs 0.55lb (250g) or less and has no exposed rotating parts that could cut skin.
EASA C0 / A1EU equivalent — C0 is the class marking for sub-250g aircraft; A1 is the lowest-risk operating subcategory, permitting flight over uninvolved individuals (not crowds).
